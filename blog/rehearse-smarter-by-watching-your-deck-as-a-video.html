Rehearse Smarter by Watching Your Deck as a Video

Most presentation rehearsal tips tell you to practise out loud, time yourself and get feedback. All good advice. But there’s one step most people skip: sitting back and watching the deck as your audience will see it. When you play your slides as a narrated video, you stop being the presenter for a few minutes and become the viewer. Problems that were invisible while you were clicking through become obvious.

This post shows how to use a video playback as a rehearsal tool, what to look for, and how to fix what you find.

Why watching beats clicking through

When you rehearse by clicking through your own slides, your brain fills in the gaps. You know what slide 7 means because you wrote it. You know why the chart matters. You know the joke lands on slide 12.

Your audience knows none of that. A video playback strips away your inside knowledge. You see:

  • Slides that sit on screen too long, or flash past too quickly.
  • Text you never mention out loud.
  • Points you make out loud with nothing on the slide to support them.
  • Transitions that feel abrupt or confusing.
  • A middle section that drags.

It’s the presentation equivalent of reading your essay aloud. You notice things your eyes skipped.

Presentation rehearsal tips: the video playback method

Here’s a simple process you can run in under an hour.

1. Write your talk into the speaker notes

Put what you plan to say in the notes for each slide. Not bullet prompts, but the actual sentences. If you usually present from memory, this step alone is useful: it shows you whether you know what you want to say.

2. Play the deck as a narrated video

Turn the deck into a video where a narrator voice reads your notes over the slides. The voice won’t sound like you, and that’s the point. You’ll hear your words as words, not as your own familiar delivery.

3. Watch once without stopping

Don’t pause. Don’t take notes yet. Just watch as an audience member would. At the end, write down the three moments where your attention drifted, and the one moment you were most confused.

4. Watch again with a notepad

This time, pause on every slide and ask the questions in the next section.

5. Fix, then watch again

Make your changes in the deck and play it again. Repeat until a full viewing feels smooth.

What to look for on each slide

Use these questions on your second viewing.

Does the slide match the words?

If the narration says “three reasons”, the slide should show three things. If the slide shows a chart, the narration should say what the chart means. A mismatch between what people hear and what they see splits their attention.

Is there text nobody reads?

If a slide has a paragraph the narration never mentions, viewers will try to read it while listening. That’s hard. Either cut the text or move it into the notes.

Is the slide on screen for the right length of time?

A slide with one line of narration may vanish before people take it in. A slide with two minutes of narration probably holds too many ideas. Split it.

Is the headline doing work?

Pause on each slide with the sound off. Does the headline alone tell you the point? “Results” doesn’t. “Response times halved after the March change” does.

Does the order make sense?

Watch for moments where you say “as we’ll see later” or “going back to”. These often mean a slide is in the wrong place.

A sample rewrite from a rehearsal

Here’s the kind of fix a playback reveals.

Before. Slide headline: “Methodology”. Slide body: five bullets about sampling, instruments, timing, ethics approval and analysis. Speaker notes:

“So for our methodology, as you can see on the slide, we used a mixed-methods approach. I won’t go through everything here but the main thing is the survey.”

On playback, the voice says “as you can see” while viewers are still reading bullet one. The note says “I won’t go through everything”, so why is it all there?

After. Split into two slides.

  • Slide A headline: “We surveyed 140 first-year students in week 4”. Body: one icon and the sample size.
  • Slide B headline: “Then interviewed 12 of them to explain the patterns”. Body: a simple two-step diagram.

New notes for Slide A:

“We started with a survey. We sent it to first-year students in week four, and a hundred and forty replied.”

Ethics approval and analysis details move into a backup slide or handout. The talk is shorter and clearer.

Timing: what the playback tells you

A narrated playback gives you an honest estimate of running time, because the voice reads every word in your notes. If the video runs well over your slot, you’ve written too much. Cut before you practise, not after.

A few timing checks:

  • Total length. Compare it to your slot, and leave room for questions and live pauses.
  • Opening. If it takes more than a minute or so to reach your first real point, tighten it. See how to open a presentation so people keep watching.
  • Section balance. If one section takes half the running time, ask whether it deserves half the attention.

Then rehearse live

The video playback doesn’t replace practising out loud. It makes your live practice better, because you’re rehearsing a tighter, clearer deck.

Once you’ve fixed the deck:

  • Practise standing up, speaking out loud, at least twice.
  • Time each run and compare it with the playback length.
  • Practise your opening and closing until you can say them without notes.
  • Do one run in front of a friend or colleague and ask one question: “What was the main point?”

If you’re preparing a talk that will be pre-recorded anyway, pre-recorded conference talks that still engage covers the next steps.

Quick checklist

  • Write your talk into the speaker notes as full sentences.
  • Watch the deck as a narrated video, start to finish, without stopping.
  • Note where your attention drifted and where you were confused.
  • Check every slide: words match visuals, no unread text, sensible time on screen, headline makes a point.
  • Use the running time to cut before you practise.
  • Rehearse live with the improved deck.
