Students: Making a Video Presentation for an Assignment

By Mal Gordon · 23 December 2026 · 5 min read

More courses now ask for a recorded presentation instead of a live one. If you have been set a video presentation for an assignment, the good news is that you can plan, fix and polish it before anyone sees it. The challenge is that the marker sees exactly what you submit, with no chance to clarify afterwards.

This playbook walks you from the brief to a finished video, using the slides and notes you would write anyway.

Start with the brief and the rubric

Before you open PowerPoint, read the assignment brief twice and the marking rubric once more. Highlight these details:

  • Time limit. Is it a maximum, a range, or a hard cut-off? Some markers stop watching at the limit.
  • Format. Does the unit accept a WebM file, or does it ask for MP4? Is there a file size limit on the submission portal?
  • Your face or voice. Some briefs require you to appear on camera or speak in your own voice. Check this first. If they do, a video narrated by a synthetic voice will not meet the requirement, and you should plan to record yourself.
  • Captions and references. Does the brief ask for a reference list slide, or captions?
  • Criteria. What earns the marks: structure, evidence, analysis, delivery?

If anything is unclear, ask your tutor early. That one email can save you a resubmission.

Plan your video presentation for an assignment

Use a simple story structure. It works for almost any topic:

  1. Situation. What is the context?
  2. Problem or question. What are you investigating?
  3. Answer. What did you find or argue?
  4. Evidence. What supports it?
  5. Next step or conclusion. What should the viewer take away?

Now map the structure to your time limit. A rough guide for spoken narration is that one slide with a few sentences of notes takes somewhere between 30 seconds and a minute. For a five-minute limit, plan around eight to ten slides, then adjust once you play it back.

Sample outline for a five-minute video

Slide Purpose Headline
1 Title “Why do first-year students drop out of group work?”
2 Situation “Group work is now in most first-year units”
3 Problem “Many students report unequal effort”
4-6 Evidence One finding per slide
7 Answer “Clear roles help groups stay together”
8 Conclusion “Three changes units could make this semester”
9 References Full reference list

Notice that each headline makes a point. That helps the marker follow your argument, which is usually part of the rubric.

Build slides that are easy to watch

Your marker may watch on a laptop or even a phone. Design for that.

  • One idea per slide.
  • Body text at 24pt or larger.
  • Strong contrast between text and background.
  • Charts with one highlighted message and direct labels.
  • Citations on the slide in a short form (author, year) with the full list at the end.
  • 16:9 slide size, so the video fills the screen.

Move detail into your speaker notes. The slide shows the evidence; the notes explain it.

Write your speaker notes as a script

Your speaker notes become your narration. Write them as you would speak, not as you would write an essay.

Essay style (hard to listen to): “Notwithstanding the aforementioned limitations, the data, which was collected via survey (n=112), indicates a statistically significant correlation between role allocation and group retention.”

Spoken style (easy to follow): “Our survey had just over a hundred responses. It has some limits, which I’ll cover shortly. But one pattern stood out. Groups that agreed on roles early were much more likely to stay together.”

A few rules that help:

  • Short sentences, one idea each.
  • Say numbers the way people say them.
  • Introduce acronyms in full the first time.
  • Avoid reading out URLs or long citations. Point to the reference slide instead.

The earlier post on how to write speaker notes that become a great voiceover goes deeper on this.

Turn the deck into a video

If your brief allows a narrated slide video, you can turn your .pptx straight into one.

  1. Save your deck as .pptx. If you built it in Google Slides, download it as Microsoft PowerPoint (.pptx). Keynote and Canva decks need exporting to .pptx first.
  2. Upload the file to PowerPoint2Vid. Your speaker notes become the narration.
  3. Pick a voice and adjust the speaking speed.
  4. Turn on burned-in captions if the brief asks for them, or if you want viewers to follow with the sound off.
  5. Watch the whole video with the sound on and check your time.
  6. Download the video.

If your course has rules about using online tools with your work, check them before you upload.

If you need to speak in your own voice, PowerPoint’s own video export lets you record narration and timings yourself. That is a better fit when the brief requires your voice.

Check against the rubric before you submit

Watch the finished video once from start to finish, with the rubric beside you.

Key takeaways

  • Read the brief for time, format and any voice or camera requirement first.
  • Plan with a story structure and one idea per slide.
  • Write notes for the ear; they become the script.
  • Watch with the sound on and check the running time.
  • Confirm the file format your portal accepts. WebM converts to MP4 with common tools if needed.
  • Submit early in case the upload is slow.
