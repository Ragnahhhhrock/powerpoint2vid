Common PowerPoint Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Most PowerPoint mistakes aren’t about taste. They’re about clarity. A slide that’s hard to read, hard to follow or trying to say too much will lose people whether it’s presented live, emailed as a PDF or turned into a video. The good news is that the same handful of problems show up again and again, and each has a simple fix.

Here are ten common mistakes, how to spot them, and what to do instead.

1. Headlines that label instead of say

The mistake: Slide titles like “Q3 Results”, “Background” or “Next Steps”. They tell people the topic, not the point.

The fix: Write the headline as a sentence that states the takeaway.

  • Before: “Customer feedback”
  • After: “Customers want faster replies more than longer hours”

If someone only reads your headlines, they should still get the story. More on this in slide headlines that make a point.

2. Too many ideas on one slide

The mistake: A slide that covers the problem, three options and a recommendation all at once.

The fix: One idea per slide. If you need two headlines, you need two slides. More slides with less on each is easier to follow than fewer, crowded slides.

3. Text that’s too small

The mistake: Body text at 12 or 14 points so everything fits.

The fix: Aim for 24pt or more for body text, especially if the deck will be watched on a laptop or phone. If it doesn’t fit at that size, there’s too much on the slide. Move the detail into the speaker notes.

4. Reading the slide word for word

The mistake: Full paragraphs on the slide, which the presenter then reads aloud. People read faster than you speak, so they finish early and tune out.

The fix: Put short phrases or a visual on the slide. Put your full sentences in the speaker notes. The slide supports what you say; it doesn’t repeat it.

5. Low contrast

The mistake: Light grey text on white, or white text over a busy photo.

The fix: Aim for WCAG AA contrast: at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Use a solid or semi-opaque panel behind text on photos. Check your colours with a contrast checker before you build a whole deck.

6. Charts with no message

The mistake: A chart pasted straight from a spreadsheet, with every series in a different colour, a legend in the corner, and no clue what to look at.

The fix:

  • Write the takeaway as the headline.
  • Highlight the one bar or line that matters; grey out the rest.
  • Label directly on the chart instead of using a legend.
  • Remove gridlines and decimals you don’t need.

7. Decoration instead of meaning

The mistake: Stock photos that don’t relate to the point, clip art, drop shadows, gradients and borders on everything.

The fix: Every element should help the viewer understand. Ask of each one: “If I removed this, would anyone miss it?” If not, remove it. White space is not wasted space.

8. Inconsistent layout

The mistake: Titles that jump around, logos in different corners, five fonts and a new colour on every slide.

The fix: Use the slide master. Set your title position, fonts, colours and logo once, and apply layouts from the master instead of formatting each slide by hand. Two fonts at most, and a small, consistent palette.

9. Animation for the sake of it

The mistake: Spinning, bouncing and flying text on every slide, or a different transition each time.

The fix: Use animation to reveal points as you talk about them, and nothing else. Pick one simple transition and use it throughout. Calm and consistent beats busy and surprising.

10. No clear ending

The mistake: The last slide says “Questions?” or “Thank you”, and the audience has no idea what you want them to do.

The fix: End with one clear next step. “Approve the pilot by Friday.” “Book a 20-minute setup call.” “Try the checklist on your next deck.” Leave that on screen.

A quick before and after

Here’s one slide showing several mistakes at once, and the fixed version.

Before

  • Headline: “Training Update”
  • Body: Seven bullets in 14pt text covering completion, feedback scores, new modules, budget and timeline.
  • A clip-art image of people at a whiteboard.
  • A different coloured bullet for each line.

After (split into two slides)

  • Slide 1 headline: “Nine in ten staff finished the new safety module”. Body: one large number and a short label.
  • Slide 2 headline: “Two new modules launch in March”. Body: a simple timeline with two points.
  • Speaker notes carry the feedback detail and budget.

Same information. Far easier to take in.

How to catch PowerPoint mistakes before anyone else does

Fixing mistakes is easy once you see them. The hard part is seeing them in your own deck.

Try these checks:

  • The headline test. Read only the headlines from start to finish. Do they tell the story?
  • The squint test. Squint at each slide. Is there one obvious place for the eye to land?
  • The sound-off test. Could someone follow the slide without you there?
  • The playback test. Play the deck as a narrated video and watch it as a viewer. Slides that sit too long, text you never mention, and abrupt jumps become obvious. See rehearse smarter by watching your deck as a video.

Quick checklist

  • Every headline states a point, not a topic.
  • One idea per slide.
  • Body text 24pt or larger.
  • Full sentences in the notes, not on the slide.
  • Contrast meets WCAG AA.
  • Every chart has one message, highlighted and labelled.
  • No decoration that doesn’t help understanding.
  • Layout set in the slide master; two fonts at most.
  • Animation only to reveal points; one consistent transition.
  • A clear call to action at the end.
