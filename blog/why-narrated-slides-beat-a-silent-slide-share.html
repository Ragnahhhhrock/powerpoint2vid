Why Narrated Slides Beat a Silent Slide Share

By Mal Gordon · 14 January 2027 · 5 min read

  • What narration adds

You finish a good call. The prospect says, “Can you send the deck?” You attach the PDF and hit send. Then it gets forwarded to a manager who wasn’t on the call, opens to slide one, and gets skimmed in a minute between meetings. A narrated presentation video changes that. The same slides, with your explanation built in, so the deck still makes sense when you’re not there to present it.

This post makes the case for narrated slides over a silent share, and shows how to put one together.

The problem with a silent slide share

Decks are built to be presented. They carry the headlines and the visuals. The reasoning, the context and the “here’s why this matters to you” live in the presenter’s head.

When you send slides on their own, that layer disappears. The reader has to guess:

  • Why this chart matters
  • Which of the three options you recommend
  • What the numbers are being compared against
  • What they’re meant to do next

People fill those gaps with assumptions, or they stop reading. The deck you worked hard on ends up doing a fraction of its job.

What narration adds

The “why” travels with the slides

A narrated presentation video carries the explanation that a silent deck leaves out. The chart comes with a voice that says, “This is the line to watch.” The pricing slide comes with, “Most teams your size start with the middle option.”

The deck explains itself to people you never met

In sales and consulting, the person who makes the decision often isn’t in the meeting. A video lets your champion forward something that does the explaining for them. They don’t have to retell your pitch from memory.

Viewers get the intended order and pace

A PDF lets readers jump to the price and close the file. A video walks them through the story you designed: the problem, the answer, the proof, then the next step. They can still skip, but the default path is the one you chose.

It’s easier to follow

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer’s work, suggests people understand more from words and pictures together than from text alone. Narration lets the slide carry the visual and the voice carry the explanation, instead of cramming both onto the screen as text.

It works for asynchronous buyers

Decision-makers are busy and spread across time zones. A short video lets them get the full story when it suits them, without booking another call. For more on that, see async video vs live meetings.

When a silent share is still fine

Narration isn’t always the answer. Send the plain deck when:

  • The recipient asked for a reference copy to pull numbers from
  • The deck is a dense appendix meant to be read, not watched
  • It’s going to someone who saw the full presentation yesterday and wants the slides

A good approach is to send both: the video for understanding and the PDF for reference.

What makes a narrated presentation video work

Not every narrated deck is worth watching. These habits make the difference.

Keep it short

Cut the deck to the slides that matter to this audience. A few minutes is usually plenty for a follow-up. Long background sections can stay in the PDF.

Write headlines that make a point

Each slide headline should state the takeaway. If the viewer only glances at the screen, they should still get the message.

Before: “Implementation”

After: “Your team is live in four weeks, with no downtime”

Write notes the way you’d say them

The narrator reads your speaker notes, so write them in short, spoken sentences. Here’s a sample for a follow-up deck:

Thanks again for the call on Tuesday. You mentioned that onboarding new staff takes too long. This slide shows how teams like yours cut that time down. The key change is the checklist on the right, which replaces the three separate forms you use now.

That note does three things: it reminds them of the conversation, ties the slide to their problem, and tells them where to look.

Finish with one clear ask

End with a single next step on the last slide and in the narration: “Reply to this email with a time for a 20-minute setup call.” One ask, not a menu.

Personalise without starting over

The strength of a narrated deck is that it’s easy to adapt. Keep a master deck. For each important prospect:

  1. Duplicate the deck
  2. Edit the opening slide and its note to name their situation
  3. Remove slides that don’t apply
  4. Adjust the closing ask
  5. Export a new video

That’s a tailored walkthrough built from slides you already maintain, without re-recording anything.

Key takeaways

  • A silent deck loses the reasoning that lives in the presenter’s head.
  • Narration keeps the “why” attached to every slide.
  • Videos reach decision-makers who were never on the call.
  • Keep it short, make headlines say the point, write notes for the ear.
  • Send the video for understanding and the PDF for reference.
