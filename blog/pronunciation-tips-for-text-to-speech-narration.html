Pronunciation Tips for Text-to-Speech Narration

Nothing breaks the flow of a training video like a narrator who says your company name wrong. Or reads “WHS” as a word. Or turns “2026-27” into a string of digits. Text to speech pronunciation problems are common, but most are easy to fix once you know what the voice is doing. The fix almost always lives in your script, not in the software.

This guide is for trainers and L&D teams turning onboarding and policy decks into narrated videos. It covers the usual trouble spots and how to rewrite them so the voice gets them right.

How text-to-speech voices read your script

A text-to-speech voice reads exactly what is written. It does not know your organisation, your product names or your internal shorthand. It guesses based on spelling and general rules.

That means:

  • Common words are usually fine.
  • Unusual names, brand terms and acronyms are where it guesses wrong.
  • Punctuation shapes the rhythm. Commas and full stops create pauses.
  • Different voices guess differently. A name one voice handles well, another may not.

Find the problem words first

Before you rewrite anything, listen. Preview the whole video with narration and note every word that sounds off. Common culprits in training decks:

  • Acronyms: WHS, HR, LMS, KPI, SOP
  • Product and system names
  • People’s names, especially from languages other than English
  • Place names
  • Numbers, dates, money and ranges
  • Units and symbols: %, &, /, km, kg
  • Words with two pronunciations depending on meaning, like “read” or “lead”

Keep a list. You will reuse it for every deck.

Text to speech pronunciation fixes that work

Acronyms: decide letters or word

Some acronyms are said as letters (H-R). Others are said as words (NATO). The voice has to guess which.

If it reads a letter acronym as a word, add spaces or full stops between the letters in the speaker notes:

  • Before: “Report it through the WHS portal.”
  • After: “Report it through the W H S portal.”

If it spells out something that should be a word, write it in lower case or as you would say it.

Even better, say the full term the first time: “Work health and safety, or W H S, covers…” That helps new staff as much as it helps the voice.

Names: spell them the way they sound

For a name the voice mangles, write a phonetic version in the speaker notes. Try a few spellings until one lands.

  • Before: “Your manager, Siobhan, will meet you on day one.”
  • After: “Your manager, Shiv-awn, will meet you on day one.”

Keep the correct spelling on the slide itself. The slide is what people read; the notes are what the voice says.

Numbers: write them the way people say them

Voices often read numbers literally. Help them out:

  • “2026-27” becomes “twenty twenty-six to twenty-seven”
  • “$1,500” becomes “fifteen hundred dollars”
  • “9am-5pm” becomes “nine a.m. to five p.m.”
  • “3/4” becomes “three quarters”
  • “1800 123 456” becomes “one eight hundred, one two three, four five six”

Phone numbers and reference codes benefit from commas, which add short pauses between groups.

Symbols and units: spell them out

Write “per cent” instead of “%”, “and” instead of “&”, “kilometres” instead of “km”. A slash can be read in odd ways, so replace it with “or” or “per”, whichever you mean.

Words with two meanings: rephrase

If the voice says “lead” like the metal when you meant “lead a team”, change the sentence. “Take charge of the team” avoids the guess. Rewriting is quicker than fighting the voice.

Jargon: ask whether you need it

If a term trips up the voice, it may trip up a new starter too. Often a plain phrase works better for both.

Fix rhythm as well as words

Pronunciation is not only about single words. A sentence that is too long sounds rushed and flat.

  • Keep sentences short. One idea each.
  • Use full stops where you want a clear pause.
  • Use commas for short breaths within a sentence.
  • Avoid brackets. The voice does not know how to say them, and the listener loses the thread.

You can also slow the speaking speed slightly for dense content, such as a compliance slide with several rules on it.

A note on captions

If you burn in captions, check how your phonetic spellings look on screen. Captions follow the narration, so “Shiv-awn” may appear as written. For a name that appears often, you may prefer to find a voice that handles the real spelling, or to rephrase so the name is on the slide and the narration says “your manager”.

Build a team pronunciation list

If several people write training decks, keep one shared list of fixes. A simple two-column document is enough:

Write on the slide Write in the speaker notes
WHS W H S
Siobhan Shiv-awn
2026-27 twenty twenty-six to twenty-seven
24/7 twenty-four seven
e.g. for example

New decks start from the list, and every video sounds consistent.

Quick checklist

  • Preview the whole video with narration and note every problem word
  • Space out letter acronyms in the speaker notes
  • Write tricky names phonetically in the notes, correctly on the slide
  • Write numbers, dates and money the way people say them
  • Spell out symbols and units
  • Rephrase words with two pronunciations
  • Keep sentences short for natural rhythm
  • Check burned-in captions for phonetic spellings
  • Test on the device and browser you will export from
  • Keep a shared pronunciation list for the team

Get the narration right, then make the video

Clear pronunciation makes a training video sound professional and keeps learners focused on the content.
