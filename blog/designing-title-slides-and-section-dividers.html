Designing Title Slides and Section Dividers

By Mal Gordon · 16 January 2027 · 5 min read

The title slide is the first thing anyone sees, and in a video it’s often the thumbnail too. Section dividers are the signposts that tell viewers where they are. Both are easy to overlook, and both shape how the rest of the deck lands. Good title slide design isn’t about decoration. It’s about telling people what they’re about to get and why it matters to them.

This post covers how to design both, with examples you can copy.

What a title slide needs to do

A title slide has three jobs:

  1. Say what this is about, in words the audience would use.
  2. Give a reason to keep watching, usually a benefit or a question.
  3. Set the tone, through type, colour and space.

It doesn’t need your logo in three places, a long subtitle, a date and a confidentiality notice in the same font size as the title.

Title slide design: write the title first

Most weak title slides have weak titles. Fix the words before you touch the layout.

Before: “Q1 Sales Kickoff 2027”

After: “Three changes that will shorten our sales cycle this year”

Before: “Module 4: Data Privacy”

After: “Handling customer data safely: what changes on 1 March”

Before: “Research Update”

After: “Why shaded playgrounds stay in use through summer”

The “after” versions tell the viewer what they’ll learn. Keep a short label like “Module 4” if your course structure needs it, but make it small and secondary.

Layout rules for title slides

One focal point

The title should be the biggest, highest-contrast thing on the slide. Everything else is supporting detail. If the eye lands on the logo or a photo first, rebalance.

Use space generously

A title slide with plenty of empty space feels confident. Resist the urge to fill corners. Centre or left-align the title, with a clear margin around it.

Limit supporting text

A good title slide usually has at most:

  • The title
  • One short subtitle or framing line
  • The presenter’s name or organisation, small

Dates, version numbers and legal notes can go in the speaker notes or on a closing slide.

Check contrast

Text on a photo or a coloured background needs strong contrast. WCAG suggests a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. If you’re placing a title over a busy image, add a solid or semi-transparent panel behind it.

Design for 16:9

Most decks are now watched on screens, not projected. Use a 16:9 layout, and make sure the title still reads when the slide is shrunk to a small preview or thumbnail.

Section dividers: signposts for the viewer

Section dividers break a longer presentation into chunks. In a live talk, they give the audience a breather. In a video, they do even more: they show progress, and they give viewers a natural place to pause or come back to later.

When to use them

  • Any deck with three or more distinct parts
  • Training modules where viewers might return to one section
  • Videos you’ll later add chapters to on platforms like YouTube

Short decks of under ten slides rarely need them.

What a good divider looks like

A section divider should be simple and consistent:

  • The section name as a clear statement
  • An optional progress marker, such as “Part 2 of 4”
  • The same layout every time, so viewers recognise it straight away

Use a different background colour or a large number to set dividers apart from content slides. Keep the style consistent across the deck.

Write dividers as mini-headlines

Before: “Section 2: Process”

After: “Part 2: How a claim moves from form to payment”

That one line tells viewers what’s coming and frames the next few slides.

Speaker notes for title and divider slides

When a deck becomes a narrated video, these slides need notes too. Otherwise they flash past or sit in silence.

A sample title slide note:

This short video covers how to handle customer data safely, and what changes on the first of March. It takes about five minutes.

A sample divider note:

Part two. How a claim moves from the online form through to payment.

Short and direct. The note tells viewers what’s next and sets expectations.

Even a one-line note keeps a divider on screen long enough to read.

A quick structure template

Slide Purpose Example
Title Topic and benefit “Three changes that will shorten our sales cycle”
Divider 1 First change “Part 1: Qualify earlier”
Divider 2 Second change “Part 2: Send a video, not a PDF”
Divider 3 Third change “Part 3: Agree next steps on every call”
Close One clear action “Pick one change to try this week”

The dividers alone tell the whole story. That’s a good sign your structure is clear. For more on building that structure, see how to structure a presentation.

Quick checklist

  • Title says what viewers will learn, in their words
  • One focal point, plenty of space
  • Supporting text kept to a line or two
  • Contrast checked against WCAG guidance
  • Dividers used for decks with three or more parts
  • Dividers consistent in layout and written as mini-headlines
  • Notes written for title and divider slides
