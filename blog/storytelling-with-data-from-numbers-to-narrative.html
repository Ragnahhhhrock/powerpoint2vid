Storytelling With Data: From Numbers to Narrative

A spreadsheet full of numbers rarely changes anyone’s mind. A clear story built from those numbers often does. A good data storytelling presentation takes the figures you already have and arranges them so the audience understands what happened, why it matters and what to do next.

This post is for anyone who presents numbers to people who have to make a decision: sales reviews, client reports, business cases and quarterly updates.

Why numbers need a narrative

When you show data without a story, the audience has to do the analysis themselves. Some will reach a different conclusion. Some will fixate on a figure that does not matter. Most will simply forget.

A narrative does three things:

  • It tells the audience what to look at.
  • It explains why it matters to them.
  • It points to what should happen next.

The numbers stay honest. You are not changing the data, just deciding the order and emphasis.

Start with the insight, not the data

Before you build a single chart, write the one sentence you want the decision-maker to remember. This is your insight.

Weak: “Here are the Q3 renewal numbers.” Strong: “Clients who completed onboarding renewed far more often than those who didn’t.”

To find the insight, ask:

  1. What changed?
  2. Compared to what?
  3. Why does that matter to this audience?
  4. What should they do about it?

If you can answer all four, you have a story.

A simple arc for a data storytelling presentation

Use the classic presentation structure, adapted for data:

  1. Situation. The baseline. “Last year, renewals were steady.”
  2. Complication. What changed. “This year, renewals dropped in one segment.”
  3. Insight. What the data reveals. “The drop was among clients who skipped onboarding.”
  4. Proof. Two or three charts that support the insight.
  5. Next step. The action you recommend. “Make onboarding part of every new contract.”

Each step can be one slide. Five to seven slides is often enough for the core story, with detail in an appendix.

Design each chart to make one point

Each chart should prove one thing. If you find yourself saying “and this chart also shows…”, split it.

Pick the right chart

Message Chart that usually works
Change over time Line chart
Comparing categories Bar chart (horizontal if labels are long)
Part of a whole Stacked bar, or a single highlighted bar
Relationship between two measures Scatter plot
A single important number Big number with a short label

Clean it up

  • Highlight the bar, line or point that matters. Grey out the rest.
  • Label values directly instead of relying on a legend.
  • Remove gridlines, 3D effects and decimal places nobody needs.
  • Write the headline as the takeaway, not the chart title.

Before: “Renewal rate by segment, FY26” After: “Onboarded clients renewed far more often”

The earlier post on designing charts people understand in five seconds goes deeper on chart clean-up.

Use comparisons to give numbers meaning

A number on its own means little. “Average response time: 4 hours.” Is that good? Compared to what?

Give every important number a reference point:

  • Against the past: “Down from a full day last year.”
  • Against a target: “Inside our six-hour promise.”
  • Against a peer group: “Faster than our other regions.”
  • In human terms: “That’s the difference between a same-day and a next-day reply.”

Human-scale comparisons are especially useful in sales and client conversations. They connect the figure to something the buyer cares about.

Write the narration around the chart

If the deck will be presented live or turned into a narrated video, the speaker notes do the storytelling. Write them to walk the viewer through the chart in order.

Sample speaker note for a renewal chart:

“Here are renewals by client group. Look at the two bars on the left. Clients who finished onboarding renewed much more often. Clients who skipped it were far less likely to stay. Same product, same price. The difference was onboarding.”

Notice the pattern:

  • Tell them what they are looking at.
  • Point them to the part that matters.
  • State the insight in plain words.
  • Rule out the obvious alternative explanation.

Say numbers the way people say them. “Just under three quarters” is easier to hear than “seventy-three point four per cent”.

Reveal data in steps

A full chart shown all at once invites the eye to wander. Building it up keeps attention on your point.

  • Show the axes and baseline first.
  • Add the comparison group.
  • Then add the highlighted bar or line.

Be honest with the data

Persuasive is not the same as misleading. Keep trust by:

  • Starting bar chart axes at zero.
  • Showing the time period and the source.
  • Mentioning limitations briefly (“small sample”, “one quarter only”).
  • Not hiding inconvenient results. Address them and explain.

A sceptical buyer or manager will look for these. Getting ahead of them strengthens your case.

Key takeaways

  • Write the insight before you build charts.
  • Use situation, complication, insight, proof, next step.
  • One chart, one point, with a headline that states it.
  • Give every key number a comparison.
  • Narrate the chart: what it is, where to look, what it means.
  • Stay honest about limits.
