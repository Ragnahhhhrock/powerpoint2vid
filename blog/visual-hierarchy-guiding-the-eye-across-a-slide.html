Visual Hierarchy: Guiding the Eye Across a Slide

When a slide appears, the viewer’s eye has to land somewhere. If everything is the same size and colour, it lands nowhere in particular and wanders. That is the problem visual hierarchy slides solve. Good hierarchy tells people what to look at first, what to read second, and what they can ignore until later. This post covers the tools that create hierarchy and how to apply them, with a worked example.

What visual hierarchy means on a slide

Visual hierarchy is the order in which people notice things. You control it with a few levers:

  • Size. Bigger things are seen first.
  • Contrast. Things that stand out from the background are noticed sooner.
  • Colour. One accent colour pulls the eye.
  • Position. People usually start near the top left in languages that read left to right.
  • Space. Elements with room around them feel more important.
  • Grouping. Things close together are read as belonging together.

A slide with strong hierarchy uses these deliberately so the viewer gets the point in the right order.

Three levels are usually enough

Most slides need only three levels:

  1. The point. The headline or the one number that matters.
  2. The support. A chart, image or a few short lines that back up the point.
  3. The detail. Labels, sources, footnotes.

If you find yourself needing a fourth or fifth level, the slide is probably trying to do too much. Split it.

The levers behind visual hierarchy slides

Size: make the point the biggest thing

Your headline should be clearly larger than anything else. A common mistake is a headline and body text that are only a couple of points apart. Make the gap obvious.

For video, keep body text at 24pt or larger, and make the headline noticeably bigger than that.

Contrast and colour: one accent, used sparingly

Pick one accent colour and use it for the single thing you want noticed: the key bar in a chart, the important number, the word that matters. Keep everything else neutral.

Use colour to support meaning, not carry it on its own. Some viewers can’t tell certain colours apart, so pair colour with a label, position or weight. Check contrast too: WCAG AA asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.

Position: put the point where the eye starts

In left-to-right languages, people tend to start near the top left. Put the headline there. Put the supporting visual where the eye naturally moves next, usually centre or right.

Space: let important things breathe

White space is not wasted space. An element with room around it reads as important. A cramped one reads as clutter. Our post on white space goes deeper on this.

Grouping: keep related things together

Put a label right next to the thing it labels. Put a caption under its image. When related items are spaced apart, the viewer has to do the matching work.

A before-and-after example

Before: A slide titled “Q3 Customer Support Metrics” in 28pt. Underneath, six bullet points in 20pt, each with a number: response time, resolution time, satisfaction score, ticket volume, backlog, and escalations. A bar chart sits on the right with all six bars in different colours. A source line sits at the bottom in the same grey as the bullets.

The eye doesn’t know where to go. Six numbers compete. Six colours compete.

After:

  • Headline (44pt, top left): “Response times halved after we added weekend cover”
  • Chart (centre right): Response time by month, all bars in grey except the months after the change, in the accent colour, with a direct label on the latest bar.
  • One supporting line (24pt): “Satisfaction held steady while ticket volume grew.”
  • Source (14pt, bottom left, light grey): Support desk data, July to September.

The other four metrics move to the speaker notes or an appendix slide. Now the eye goes headline, chart, supporting line, source. In that order, every time.

Visual hierarchy in slides that become video

In video, hierarchy works over time as well as across space.

  • Animate in order of importance. If shapes appear one by one, the headline should come first, then the support, then the detail. That reinforces the hierarchy you designed.
  • Match the narration to the hierarchy. The speaker notes should talk about the point first, then the support. If the voice talks about a detail the eye hasn’t reached yet, viewers get lost.
  • Give the point time. Let the headline settle before the next element arrives.

A sample speaker note for the “after” slide:

After we added weekend cover, our response times halved. You can see the drop from August onwards. And satisfaction held steady, even as ticket volume grew.

The narration follows the same order as the slide: point, then chart, then the supporting line.

A quick hierarchy test

Try these on any slide:

  • The squint test. Squint at the slide. What still stands out? It should be the point.
  • The five-second test. Show the slide to someone for five seconds, then hide it. Ask what it was about. If they can’t say, the hierarchy needs work.
  • The phone test. Look at the slide at phone size. If the headline isn’t still the obvious first thing, make it bigger.

Quick checklist

  • One clear point per slide, shown biggest.
  • Three levels at most: point, support, detail.
  • One accent colour, used for the thing that matters.
  • Contrast that meets WCAG AA.
  • Related items grouped; labels next to what they label.
  • Animation and narration in the same order as the hierarchy.
