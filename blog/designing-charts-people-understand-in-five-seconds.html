Designing Charts People Understand in Five Seconds

A chart on a slide has a few seconds to do its job. In a live meeting, you can point at the bar that matters. In a video or a forwarded deck, you cannot. The chart has to explain itself. Most charts in presentations fail not because the data is wrong, but because they show everything and say nothing.

This guide shows how to design charts that a buyer, a manager or a student understands at a glance. The aim is simple: one chart, one message, five seconds.

Start with the sentence, not the chart

Before you open the chart tool, write the one sentence you want people to take away. For example:

  • “Renewals rose every quarter after we changed onboarding.”
  • “Support tickets fell by about half in the first month.”
  • “Region B is now our largest market.”

If you cannot write the sentence, you are not ready to make the chart. The sentence decides the chart type, what to highlight and what to cut.

Charts in presentations: pick the right type

Match the chart to the kind of point you are making.

Your point is about Use Avoid
Change over time Line chart Pie chart
Comparing a few items Bar chart, sorted 3D bars
Part of a whole, two or three parts Simple bar or a single stacked bar Pie with many slices
A single key number The number itself, large A chart at all
Ranking Horizontal bar, largest at top Unsorted bars

When in doubt, use a bar chart. People read lengths along a common baseline easily.

And remember the last row. Sometimes the best chart is no chart. If the point is “We saved 40 hours a month”, put “40 hours a month” on the slide in large type.

Highlight the one thing that matters

This is the single biggest improvement you can make.

  1. Make every bar or line grey.
  2. Colour only the one that carries your message.
  3. Use your brand’s accent colour for it.

The eye goes straight to the colour. The grey gives context without competing. Avoid relying on colour alone, though. Add a label or a short note beside the highlighted item so people with colour vision deficiency get the same message.

Label directly and cut the clutter

Every element on a chart should earn its place. Remove:

  • Legends, when you can label the lines or bars directly
  • Gridlines, or make them very light
  • Axis titles that repeat what the headline says
  • Decimal places nobody needs
  • 3D effects, shadows and gradients
  • Data labels on every bar, when only one value matters

Then add back only what helps:

  • A direct label on the highlighted bar or line
  • A clear value on the item that matters
  • A short note explaining a spike or drop, if there is one

Write a headline that states the takeaway

The chart headline should say what the chart shows, not what it is.

  • Before: “Quarterly renewals FY26”
  • After: “Renewals rose every quarter after the new onboarding”

The viewer reads the headline, looks at the chart, and sees the proof. That is the five-second test passing. For more headline examples, see Slide Headlines That Make a Point.

A before and after example

Before. A slide titled “Pipeline by region”. A pie chart with seven slices in seven colours. A legend on the right. Percentages on every slice in small type. The speaker says, “As you can see, Region B is doing well.”

After. The headline reads “Region B is now our largest pipeline”. A horizontal bar chart, sorted largest first. Six grey bars and one bar in the brand accent colour for Region B, labelled directly with its value. No legend. No gridlines.

Speaker notes for the narrated version:

“Region B is now our largest pipeline. It overtook Region A this quarter. The other regions are steady. So our next hire goes to Region B.”

The chart shows the point. The narration explains why it matters and what happens next.

Make charts work in video

When a deck becomes a narrated video, a few extra habits help.

  • Size up. Labels and values should be readable on a laptop or phone. Aim for the same 24pt minimum you use for body text where you can.
  • Give it time. A chart needs a little longer on screen than a headline slide. Write enough narration in the speaker notes to cover it.
  • Build it up. In the video, your charts are rebuilt as animated charts with the same numbers, so the context can appear first and the highlight last, landing as the narration names it.
  • Say the number the way people say it. Write “about half” or “forty thousand” in the notes, not “49.7%” or “40,000”.
  • Check contrast. Grey bars on a grey background disappear. Use a light grey on a white background, or similar.

Quick checklist

  • Write the one-sentence takeaway first
  • Choose the chart type that fits the point
  • Grey everything except the one item that matters
  • Label directly instead of using a legend
  • Remove gridlines, 3D effects and extra decimals
  • Put the takeaway in the headline
  • Make labels large enough for a small screen
  • Give the chart enough narration time in the video
