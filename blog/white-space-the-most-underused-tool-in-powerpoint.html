White Space: The Most Underused Tool in PowerPoint

PowerPoint white space is the empty area around and between things on a slide. Most people treat it as wasted room and fill it. That instinct makes slides harder to read. Space is what tells the eye where to look, what belongs together and what matters most. Adding it takes nothing more than a little discipline.

This post explains what white space does, how to find more of it in a crowded deck, and how to keep it consistent.

What white space actually does

White space doesn’t have to be white. It is any area without content: a dark background, a calm part of a photo, a margin. It does three jobs.

  1. Focus. When one element sits in open space, the eye goes straight to it.
  2. Grouping. Things close together look related. Things far apart look separate. You can organise a slide without boxes or lines just by adjusting gaps.
  3. Pace. A calm slide feels confident. A crowded one feels rushed, even before anyone reads a word.

Why crowded slides happen

Slides fill up for understandable reasons:

  • The deck doubles as a handout, so everything gets written on the slide.
  • Someone asks “can we add the logo, the date and the disclaimer to every slide?”
  • Templates come with decorative bars, shapes and footers that eat space.
  • People worry that an empty area looks unfinished.

The result is a slide where every element competes, and nothing wins.

How to add PowerPoint white space to an existing deck

You don’t need to redesign from scratch. Work through these steps on each slide.

1. Remove what doesn’t help

Start by deleting:

  • Repeated logos (keep one on the title and closing slides).
  • Footers that repeat the deck title.
  • Decorative lines and shapes with no meaning.
  • Bullet points that restate the headline.

2. Move detail into speaker notes

If text is there to help you remember what to say, it belongs in the notes, not on the slide. This is the single biggest source of new space in most decks.

3. Set generous margins

Keep content away from the edges. A consistent margin on all four sides makes every slide feel deliberate. If you’re making a video, generous margins also stop text getting too close to the frame edge or to burned-in captions.

4. Increase the gap between groups

Look for elements that belong together, such as a chart and its caption, and pull them close. Then push unrelated groups further apart. Gaps do the work that boxes and borders used to.

5. Align everything to a few lines

Pick a left edge, a top edge and perhaps a centre line. Snap everything to them. Aligned layouts make the leftover space look intentional instead of accidental.

A before-and-after example

Before

  • Headline: “Q3 Update”
  • Logo top left, logo bottom right
  • Seven bullets in 16pt text running edge to edge
  • A small bar chart squeezed into the bottom right corner
  • A footer with the date, page number and “Confidential”

After

  • Headline: “New customers doubled after we simplified sign-up”
  • The bar chart, large, on the left two-thirds of the slide, with the latest bar highlighted
  • One line of supporting text on the right: “Sign-up went from five steps to two”
  • Generous margins on all sides
  • The bullets moved into speaker notes:

In the third quarter, new customer numbers roughly doubled. The main change was sign-up. We cut it from five steps to two, and more people finished it.

The slide carries less, but says more. The viewer sees one point, one chart and one supporting line.

Spacing guide

Element Practical approach
Outer margin Keep it consistent on every slide and wide enough that nothing feels jammed against the edge
Headline to body Leave a clear gap so the headline reads as its own unit
Between groups Make it noticeably larger than the gap within groups
Line spacing in body text A little more than the default often reads better on screen
Around images Let pictures bleed off the edge or give them clear space, but don’t half-touch the margin

White space in slide templates

The best time to fix spacing is in the slide master. Set your margins, title position and body placeholders once, and every new slide inherits them. Check that:

  • Placeholders sit inside your chosen margins.
  • Decorative elements are minimal or removed.
  • There’s a layout for a single big image and one for a single big number.

White space and video

Space matters more when a deck becomes a video. Viewers can’t lean closer or flip back. A crowded slide may also lose detail when the video is compressed, while a clean one stays crisp.

Space also gives motion room to work. Animated reveals, slow push-ins and gentle transitions look calm on a spacious slide and chaotic on a full one. If you plan to burn in captions, leave the lower part of the slide clear so captions don’t sit on top of your content.

Key takeaways

  • White space is any empty area, and it guides attention.
  • Delete repeated and decorative elements first.
  • Move reminders and detail into speaker notes.
  • Use consistent margins and alignment.
  • Group with gaps, not boxes.
  • Set spacing once in the slide master.
  • Leave room at the bottom for captions in video.
