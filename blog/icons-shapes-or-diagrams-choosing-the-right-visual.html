Icons, Shapes or Diagrams? Choosing the Right Visual

By Mal Gordon · 12 December 2026 · 5 min read

Training decks often hit the same wall. The content is solid, but every slide is a list. Someone suggests “making it more visual”, and a pile of icons appears. The slides look busier, but they are not any clearer. Choosing between icons, shapes and presentation diagrams is a simple decision once you know what each one is for.

Here is the short version. Icons label. Shapes group and emphasise. Diagrams explain relationships. Pick the one that matches what you are trying to say.

Start with the message, not the visual

Before you open the icon library, write the slide’s point as one sentence. Then ask what kind of point it is.

  • A set of separate items? “There are four types of leave.”
  • A sequence? “Report a hazard in three steps.”
  • A relationship? “Managers approve, HR records, payroll pays.”
  • A comparison? “Contractors and employees have different obligations.”
  • A hierarchy? “Policies sit above procedures, which sit above work instructions.”

The answer tells you which visual to use. A set of items might only need icons. A sequence or a relationship needs a diagram.

When icons help

Icons are labels. They help people recognise categories quickly, especially when the same categories return across a course.

Use icons when:

  • You have three to six parallel items of equal weight.
  • The same categories appear on several slides and you want a visual shorthand.
  • Each icon has an obvious meaning, like a calendar for dates or a shield for security.

Avoid icons when:

  • The meaning is abstract and the icon needs its own explanation.
  • You are mixing styles, such as outline icons next to filled ones.
  • The icon is the only thing carrying meaning. Always pair it with a word.

Keep icons the same size, the same style and the same colour. Consistency is what makes them read as a system rather than decoration.

When shapes are enough

Plain shapes do a lot of quiet work. A rectangle groups related text. A circle draws the eye to a number. A thin line separates two ideas.

Shapes are the right tool when you need structure without adding meaning. For example, two rounded rectangles side by side, one labelled “Before” and one “After”, turn a dense comparison into something the eye can scan.

A few rules keep shapes tidy:

  • Use one or two shape types per deck.
  • Align them to a grid so edges line up.
  • Use colour to show emphasis, not to decorate. Grey for context, one accent colour for the thing that matters.

For more on guiding the eye, see Visual Hierarchy: Guiding the Eye Across a Slide.

When you need presentation diagrams

Diagrams earn their place when the relationship between items is the point. If you remove the arrows and the message falls apart, you need a diagram.

Message type Diagram that fits Training example
Sequence Process flow, left to right Steps to report a safety incident
Cycle Circular flow Annual performance review cycle
Hierarchy Tree or pyramid Policy, procedure, work instruction
Comparison Two columns or a matrix Employee vs contractor obligations
Decision Simple flowchart with yes/no Do I need approval for this purchase?
Overlap Two or three circles Where privacy and security policies meet

Keep diagrams simple. Five boxes is usually the limit before people lose track. If you need more, split the diagram across two slides, or show the full picture first and then zoom into one part.

A before and after example

Here is a typical compliance slide.

Before

Title: Incident reporting

  • Make the area safe if you can do so without risk
  • Tell your supervisor as soon as possible
  • Complete the incident form within 24 hours
  • Supervisor reviews and escalates if required

After

Title: Report an incident in four steps

A left-to-right process with four boxes: Make safe, then Tell supervisor, then Lodge form (24 hrs), then Supervisor reviews. Each box has a short label. The detail, such as what “safe” means and where to find the form, moves into the speaker notes.

Sample speaker note for that slide:

“There are four steps when something goes wrong. First, make the area safe, but only if you can do that without putting yourself at risk. Second, tell your supervisor straight away. Third, fill in the incident form within twenty-four hours. Finally, your supervisor reviews it and escalates if needed.”

The slide shows the shape of the process. The narration fills in the detail.

Making diagrams work in video

Diagrams become even more useful when the deck is turned into a narrated video, because you can reveal each part as it is mentioned.

  • Build the diagram in order. In PowerPoint2Vid, the “animate shapes in one by one” option brings shapes in individually, so a process diagram can appear step by step as the narration moves along.
  • Check the order in preview. Watch the slide build with voice before exporting. If a box arrives before the narration mentions it, adjust the deck or the notes until the two line up.
  • Keep labels large. Small text inside boxes is hard to read on a phone. Shorten the label rather than shrinking the font.
  • Match the notes to the diagram. Describe the steps in the same order they appear.

Charts follow similar rules. See Designing Charts People Understand in Five Seconds for the data version of this advice.

Key takeaways

  • Write the slide’s point as one sentence before choosing a visual.
  • Icons label categories. Keep them consistent and always pair them with words.
  • Shapes group and emphasise. Use few types and align them.
  • Diagrams show relationships: sequences, cycles, hierarchies, comparisons, decisions.
  • Keep diagrams to about five elements, and move detail into speaker notes.
  • In video, reveal diagram parts in the order you talk about them.
