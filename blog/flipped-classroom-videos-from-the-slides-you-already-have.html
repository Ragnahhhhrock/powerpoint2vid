Flipped Classroom Videos From the Slides You Already Have

By Mal Gordon · 18 November 2026 · 5 min read

The flipped classroom idea is simple. Students meet the content before class, then use class time for discussion, problem-solving and practice. The hard part is making the pre-class material. Recording lectures takes hours, and a reading list is easy to skip. Flipped classroom videos made from the slides you already have are a practical middle path.

This playbook shows lecturers, teachers and learning designers how to turn existing lecture slides into short pre-class videos, without a studio or a microphone.

Why slides make good flipped classroom videos

You already have the raw material. Most courses carry years of lecture slides, refined each semester. They are sequenced, they match the learning outcomes, and they use the terms students will see in assessments.

Turning them into narrated videos gives students:

  • Control over pace. They can pause, rewind and rewatch the parts they find hard.
  • Words and pictures together. Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer’s work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone.
  • Captions. Helpful for students who are deaf or hard of hearing, studying in a second language, or watching on a bus.
  • Consistency. Every student and every tutorial group gets the same explanation.

Plan short videos, not recorded lectures

The most common mistake is to turn a full lecture into one long video. Pre-class videos work best when they are short and focused.

Break the lecture into chunks

Look at your lecture slides and find the natural sections. Each section usually covers one concept. Each concept becomes one video.

For example, a week on supply and demand might become:

  1. What a demand curve shows
  2. What shifts demand
  3. What a supply curve shows
  4. Where supply meets demand

Four short videos are easier to watch, easier to find again and easier to update than one long one.

Decide what stays for class

The flipped model only works if class time is different. For each video, write down what students will do with the idea in class. A problem set, a case discussion, a debate. If you cannot name an activity, the content might be better delivered in class itself.

Prepare the slides for video

Lecture slides are often built for a live room, where you can point and expand. A few changes help them work on screen.

  • Trim the text. Move explanations into the speaker notes. Keep the slide for the key term, diagram or example.
  • Make headlines that state the idea. “Demand falls when price rises” teaches more than “Demand”.
  • Check contrast and font size. Aim for body text of 24pt or more and WCAG AA contrast (4.5:1 for normal text). For more on this, see Contrast and Accessibility in Slide Design.
  • Remove slides that only work live. “Any questions?” and poll slides can go.

Write speaker notes students can follow

In PowerPoint2Vid, the speaker notes become the narration, read aloud as the narration. Write them for the ear.

Here is an example for a slide titled “Demand falls when price rises”:

“Here’s the first idea for this week. When the price of something goes up, people usually buy less of it. That’s what this downward slope shows. Think about your morning coffee. If the price doubled, you might still buy it, but probably not every day. Keep that example in mind. We’ll use it in class on Tuesday.”

Notice the structure:

  • One idea, stated plainly
  • A familiar example
  • A link to what happens in class

That last line matters. It tells students why they are watching and what they will do next.

Tips for narration scripts

  • Keep sentences short.
  • Spell out abbreviations the first time.
  • Write numbers and symbols as words.
  • Preview with narration and fix any term the voice mispronounces.
  • Adjust the speaking speed if dense content feels rushed.

Build the video

  1. Save your slides as .pptx. If you use Google Slides, download the deck as a Microsoft PowerPoint file first. Old .ppt files need saving as .pptx.
  2. Add the file to PowerPoint2Vid.
  3. Check your speaker notes. They become the narration.
  4. Choose a voice and speaking speed, and a look and feel. Clean and calm suits most teaching content; something warmer can suit humanities topics.
  5. Include captions.
  6. Review the video, then download it as an MP4 (WebM is also available).

The animated scenes help here: key points land on the word that names them, so students can follow along without hunting the slide.

Put the videos where students will find them

Upload each video to your LMS, such as Moodle, Canvas or Blackboard, or to the video platform your institution uses. Then place it in the week’s module, right next to the activity that depends on it.

A simple pattern for each week:

Before class In class After class
Watch two or three short videos Apply the ideas in a task or discussion Short quiz or reflection

Tell students how long the videos are in total. “Twelve minutes of video before Tuesday” is a clear, reasonable ask.

Quick checklist

  • Split each lecture into one-concept chunks
  • Name the class activity each video prepares students for
  • Trim slide text and move explanations into the notes
  • Write notes for the ear, with a link to class time
  • Check font size and contrast
  • Include captions
  • Upload to the LMS beside the related activity
  • Tell students the total viewing time

Start with one week

You do not need to flip a whole course at once. Pick one week, take the slides you already have, and make two or three short videos.
