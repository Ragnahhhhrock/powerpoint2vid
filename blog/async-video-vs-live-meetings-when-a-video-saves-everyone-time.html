Async Video vs Live Meetings: When a Video Saves Everyone Time

A lot of meetings are really presentations with a calendar invite. One person shares their screen, walks through slides, and everyone else listens. If that’s all that happens, the meeting probably didn’t need to be live. Async video communication lets you send the presentation instead, so people watch when it suits them and the live time goes to the part that actually needs a conversation.

This post looks at when a video saves everyone time, when a meeting is still the right call, and how to make the switch without losing the relationship.

What async video communication actually means

“Async” just means not at the same time. You record or create a video, send it, and the other person watches it later. They can pause, rewind, skip ahead or watch at a faster speed. Then they reply in their own time, or come to a shorter meeting already up to speed.

For sales, customer success and consulting, this often takes the form of:

  • A narrated pitch deck sent after a first call
  • A product walkthrough for a new customer
  • A short project update for a client
  • A proposal summary for stakeholders who missed the meeting

When a video beats a meeting

A video tends to work better when the content is mostly one-way.

The information is the same for everyone

If you give the same product overview to every prospect, you’re repeating yourself. A well-made video gives every prospect the same clear explanation, and saves your live time for their specific questions.

Several people need it, at different times

Buying decisions usually involve more than one person. The champion you met might need to brief their manager, finance and IT. A video can be forwarded to each of them, so your pitch reaches people who were never in the room, explained the way you’d explain it.

Time zones get in the way

Finding a time that works across Perth, Sydney and Singapore is hard. Add London and it gets harder. A video arrives in their morning, whenever that is.

People need to think before they respond

Complex proposals and pricing options benefit from a bit of reflection. A video lets people take it in, rewatch the tricky part, and come back with considered questions.

When to keep the meeting

Async isn’t always better. Keep the live meeting when:

  • You’re building a new relationship. First calls benefit from real conversation.
  • The topic is sensitive. Bad news, pricing disputes or complaints deserve a human voice in real time.
  • You need a decision on the spot. If the goal is agreement, and agreement depends on debate, meet.
  • The discussion is the point. Brainstorming and problem-solving need back and forth.

A good rule: if the meeting is mostly you talking, send a video. If it’s mostly them talking, meet.

A simple comparison

Situation Better as Why
Standard product overview Video Same content every time
Follow-up for stakeholders who missed the call Video Reaches people on their schedule
Weekly status update Video One-way, short, easy to skim
Discovery call Meeting You need to listen and ask
Negotiation Meeting Needs real-time back and forth
Delivering bad news Meeting Needs a human, live
Walkthrough before a decision meeting Video, then meeting Everyone arrives prepared

That last row is often the best of both. Send the video first, then hold a shorter meeting focused on questions and next steps.

How to make an async video people actually watch

A video only saves time if people watch it. A few habits help.

  1. Keep it short. A few minutes is easier to commit to than half an hour. Cover one topic.
  2. Say why it matters in the first slide. “This shows how we’d cut your reporting time, in four minutes.”
  3. Use headlines that make a point. If someone skims, the slide titles alone should tell the story.
  4. End with one clear next step. “Reply with a time that suits for a twenty-minute call.”
  5. Write a short message to go with it. Two or three lines explaining what’s in the video and how long it is.

Example: replacing a follow-up meeting

The old way: After a good discovery call, you book another meeting to present your proposal to the wider team. It takes a week to find a time. Two people can’t make it.

The async way: You send a five-minute narrated deck the next morning.

  • Slide 1 headline: “Three changes that would halve your month-end reporting time”
  • Slide 2: What you heard on the call, in their words
  • Slides 3 to 5: One slide per change, each with a simple visual
  • Slide 6: Cost and timeline
  • Slide 7: “Next step: a twenty-minute call to answer questions”

Sample speaker notes for slide 1:

Thanks for the time on Tuesday. This short video covers what we heard and what we’d suggest. There are three changes. Together, we think they’d cut your month-end reporting time in half. I’ll walk through each one, and then the cost and timing.

The message: “Here’s a five-minute walkthrough of what we discussed. Please share it with anyone else who should see it. Happy to answer questions on a short call next week.”

The champion forwards it to finance and IT the same day. The next meeting is about questions, not a presentation.

Key takeaways

  • If a meeting is mostly one person presenting, consider sending a video.
  • Video works well for repeated content, multiple stakeholders and time zones.
  • Keep meetings for relationships, sensitive topics, debate and decisions.
  • Video first, then a shorter meeting, often gets the best of both.
  • Short, focused videos with a clear next step get watched.
