How to Write Speaker Notes That Become a Great Voiceover

By Mal Gordon · 2 November 2026 · 5 min read

Most speaker notes are written for one person: the presenter, glancing down mid-talk. They’re full of fragments, abbreviations and reminders. That’s fine on stage. But when your notes become the voiceover for a video, they need to work for a listener who has never seen them. Good speaker notes narration starts with writing notes that sound right when read aloud.

This guide shows you how to turn rough notes into a script, slide by slide, so your recorded talk, assignment video or research explainer sounds clear and natural.

Why speaker notes make the best voiceover source

Your speaker notes sit right next to each slide. That makes them the natural home for narration. The words for slide 4 live on slide 4. When you reorder slides, the script moves with them. When you fix a slide, the fix to the script is right there.

Compare that to a separate script document. It drifts out of sync the moment you change the deck. Keeping the script in the notes avoids that problem completely.

The difference between notes and a script

Here’s a typical set of notes for a research slide:

Results - 3 groups - control flat - group B big jump wk 4 - mention outlier

And here’s the same content written as a script:

Let’s look at the results. We tested three groups. The control group stayed flat the whole time. Group B is where it gets interesting. Look at week four. That’s where scores jump. There’s one outlier here, and I’ll come back to it on the next slide.

The script is longer. It also does things the notes can’t:

  • It uses full sentences a voice can read.
  • It tells the viewer where to look.
  • It links to the next slide, so the talk flows.

Six rules for speaker notes narration

1. Write the way you speak

Read your draft aloud. If you wouldn’t say it in conversation, rewrite it. “Utilise” becomes “use”. “In order to” becomes “to”. “It should be noted that” disappears.

2. Keep sentences short

Aim for one idea per sentence. A listener can’t scan back up the page. Long sentences with several clauses are hard to follow by ear, and harder still for a voice to phrase well.

3. Spell out what the voice might trip on

Abbreviations, symbols and numbers can come out oddly. Write them the way you want them heard.

Written on slide Write in notes as
3.5% three and a half per cent
Q3 FY26 the third quarter of the 2026 financial year
e.g. for example
n=240 two hundred and forty participants
vs versus, or compared with

4. Point to the slide

Your viewer is looking at the slide while listening. Use that. Say “on the left”, “the orange bar”, “this second point”. It ties the voice to the visual and keeps attention in the right place.

5. Don’t read the slide word for word

If the slide says “Sample size: 240”, the narration shouldn’t just say “sample size, two hundred and forty”. Say why it matters: “We had two hundred and forty participants, which is enough to see a clear difference between groups.”

The slide shows the fact. The voice explains it.

6. Match the length to the slide

A title slide might need one sentence. A dense results slide might need six. As a rough guide, most content slides land well with three to six short sentences. If you need much more, the slide probably holds two ideas and should be split.

Use punctuation to shape the pace

Narration takes its cues from punctuation. A full stop gives a short pause. A comma gives a smaller one. You can use this to control pacing without any special markup.

  • Want a beat before a key number? End the sentence before it.
  • Want a list to sound like a list? Use commas between items.
  • Want emphasis? Put the important phrase in its own short sentence.

Before:

Our model was accurate in most cases and the main exception was low-light images where accuracy dropped significantly.

After:

Our model was accurate in most cases. There was one exception. Low-light images. That’s where accuracy dropped.

The second version gives the listener time to absorb each point. There’s more on voice selection and speed in choosing a narrator voice and speaking speed.

A full worked example

Here’s one slide from a conference talk, with notes written for narration.

Slide headline: “Shorter onboarding cut drop-offs in the first week”

Slide visual: A line chart with two lines, old onboarding and new onboarding.

Speaker notes:

Here’s what happened when we shortened onboarding. The grey line is the old process. The blue line is the new one. Notice the first week. With the old process, many people left before day seven. With the new one, far more of them stayed. That first week is where the change made the biggest difference.

It’s six sentences. It points to the chart, explains the colours, tells the viewer what to notice, and states the takeaway at the end.

What to do with slides that have no notes

Some slides don’t need narration: a section divider, a quote, a closing contact slide. You have a few options:

  • Add a one-line note like “Part two: the results.” It gives the slide a voice and keeps the rhythm going.
  • Leave it empty if the slide speaks for itself, and give it a moment of silence on screen.
  • Keep the visual strong so a slide with little narration still holds attention.

Quick checklist

  • Every content slide has notes written in full sentences
  • Sentences are short, one idea each
  • Numbers, symbols and abbreviations are written as spoken words
  • Notes point to what’s on the slide
  • Notes explain, rather than repeat, the slide text
  • Each set of notes links to the next slide where it helps
  • Read aloud once before converting
