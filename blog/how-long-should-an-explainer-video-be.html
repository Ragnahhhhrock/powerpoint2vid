How Long Should an Explainer Video Be?

“How long should it be?” is usually the first question people ask about an explainer video. The honest answer is: as short as it can be while still doing its job. The right explainer video length depends on what the video is for, who’s watching and where they’ll find it. This guide gives you a practical way to decide, plus techniques for cutting a script down to size.

Why there’s no single right explainer video length

You’ll see confident claims online that every explainer should be exactly 60 or 90 seconds. Real use is messier. A teaser on social media and a step-by-step onboarding video have very different jobs.

Three things decide the length:

  • The goal. Spark interest, explain a concept, or teach a task?
  • The viewer’s intent. Did they stumble across it, or go looking for it?
  • The platform. A fast-moving feed is different from a help centre or course page.

A viewer who searched “how do I export my report” will happily watch three minutes if it solves their problem. A stranger scrolling a feed may give you a few seconds.

Rough ranges by purpose

These are starting points, not rules. Use them to plan, then let the content decide.

Purpose Typical starting point Why
Social teaser or ad Under a minute Viewers didn’t come looking; get to the point fast
Home page or product explainer One to two minutes Enough to state the problem, the answer and the next step
Sales or pitch walkthrough Two to four minutes The viewer is interested and wants substance
Product walkthrough or how-to As long as the task needs, one task per video Viewers are following along and want completeness
Course or lecture segment Short segments rather than one long recording Easier to watch, review and update

If a video creeps past the upper end of its range, consider splitting it.

How to estimate length before you produce anything

For narrated videos, the script decides the length. You can estimate it before making anything.

  1. Write the script. For slide videos, that’s your speaker notes.
  2. Read it aloud at a natural pace and time it. This is the most reliable method.
  3. Add a little for pauses and transitions between slides.

You can adjust the speaking speed, but resist speeding up to fit a target length. Faster narration is harder to follow. Cut words instead.

How to cut a script without losing the message

Most first drafts are too long. Here’s how to trim.

Cut the warm-up

Openings like “Hi, in this video we’re going to take a look at…” use time without saying anything. Start with the problem or the point.

One idea per slide, one sentence per idea

If a slide’s notes run to a long paragraph, look for the one sentence that carries the point. Keep it. Move the rest to a follow-up video, an article or a handout.

Remove what the slide already shows

If the slide shows “Orders ship within 24 hours”, the narration doesn’t need to read it word for word and then explain it again. Add something the slide doesn’t say.

Drop the nice-to-knows

Ask of each sentence: would the viewer miss this if it were gone? If not, cut it.

A before-and-after example

Before (speaker notes for one slide):

Hi everyone, and welcome. Today I’d like to take a few minutes to walk you through our new scheduling feature, which is something our team has been working on for quite some time now and which we think is going to make a really big difference to how you plan your week. So let’s jump in and take a look at how it all works.

After:

Planning your week now takes one screen. Here’s how the new scheduler works.

The second version takes a fraction of the time, and the viewer knows exactly what’s coming.

Signs your video is too long

  • The main point arrives after the first few slides.
  • You repeat the same idea in different words.
  • There’s more than one call to action.
  • You find yourself wanting to speed up the voice.
  • A colleague watches it and can’t say what it was about.

Signs it’s too short

Short is good, but not at the cost of clarity. Your video may be too short if:

  • Viewers ask the same questions after watching.
  • Steps are skipped or rushed in a how-to.
  • Slides flash past before people can read them.

In slide videos, giving each slide enough narration prevents that last problem. Our post on how long each slide should stay on screen covers slide-level timing in detail.

A series often beats one long video

When content keeps growing, split it. Three focused two-minute videos are easier to find, watch and update than one long one. Each can have its own clear title and call to action. Viewers can jump straight to the part they need.

Key takeaways

  • The right explainer video length depends on the goal, the viewer’s intent and the platform.
  • Use rough ranges to plan, then let the script decide.
  • Time your script aloud before producing anything.
  • Cut words rather than speeding up the voice.
  • When a video grows too long, split it into a series.
