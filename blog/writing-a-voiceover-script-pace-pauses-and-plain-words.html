Writing a Voiceover Script: Pace, Pauses and Plain Words

A voiceover script is a strange thing to write. It’s text, but nobody reads it. They only hear it, once, at the speed it’s spoken. That changes the rules. The voiceover script tips in this guide focus on three things that make the biggest difference: pace, pauses and plain words.

Whether you’re preparing a pre-recorded conference talk, an assignment video or a research explainer, these habits will make your narration easier to follow.

Why scripts for the ear are different

When people read, they control the pace. They slow down on a hard sentence, skip back to check a number, glance ahead to the next heading. A listener can do none of that. The script decides the pace for them.

So a good voiceover script does more of the work:

  • It breaks ideas into small pieces.
  • It repeats key points in slightly different words.
  • It signals structure out loud: “There are three reasons. The first…”
  • It avoids anything that needs re-reading to understand.

Voiceover script tips for pace

Pace is how quickly ideas arrive, not just how fast the voice speaks. You can slow a script down or speed it up through the writing itself.

Use sentence length as a dial

Short sentences slow things down and add weight. Longer sentences carry the listener along. Mix them deliberately.

We expected the new process to save time. It didn’t. In the first month, the average task actually took longer, because people were still learning where everything was.

The short middle sentence lands hard because it sits between two longer ones.

Give one idea per sentence

A sentence with two ideas forces the listener to hold the first while processing the second. Split them.

Before: “The survey, which ran over three weeks and reached staff in all four offices, showed that most people preferred hybrid work, although managers were more divided.”

After: “The survey ran for three weeks. It reached staff in all four offices. Most people preferred hybrid work. Managers were more divided.”

Set the speaking speed after the script

You can choose a voice and adjust the speaking speed. Get the script right first, then tune the speed. A well-written script usually sounds best at a natural, moderate rate. If it only sounds clear when slowed right down, the sentences are probably too long. For more, see choosing a narrator voice and speaking speed.

Using pauses on purpose

Pauses give listeners time to absorb what they’ve just heard. They also signal that something important is coming.

Punctuation is your pause control

Narration responds to punctuation:

  • Full stop: a clear pause. Use it before and after key points.
  • Comma: a short breath. Use it to separate list items and clauses.
  • Question mark: a change in tone and a pause. Useful for setting up an answer.
  • New paragraph: often a slightly longer gap in practice, and a useful way to organise your own thinking.

Pause before the number, not after

People remember the thing that follows a pause. If you want a figure to stick, set it up.

So how many participants finished the study? Just over half.

Let the slide breathe

When a new slide appears, the viewer needs a moment to take it in. Start the narration with a short orienting sentence (“Here’s the timeline.”) rather than diving straight into detail.

Plain words that people follow

Spoken jargon is harder to decode than written jargon, because there’s no time to stop and work it out.

Instead of Try
utilise use
facilitate help
in the event that if
a significant proportion of many, or most
prior to before
leverage use, or build on
going forward from now on

Define terms once, simply

If you need a technical term, define it the first time in plain words, then use it consistently.

We used a control group. That’s a group that didn’t get the new treatment, so we had something to compare against.

Write numbers the way they’re said

Write “about two thirds” rather than “66.7%” if precision doesn’t matter. When it does, spell out how it should sound: “sixty-six point seven per cent”. The same goes for dates, currencies and abbreviations.

Signposting: tell people where they are

Listeners can’t see the structure of your script, so say it out loud.

  • Preview: “I’ll cover three things: the problem, what we tried, and what we found.”
  • Transition: “That’s the problem. Now, what did we try?”
  • Recap: “So, to sum up. The problem was X. We tried Y. And the result was Z.”

It feels repetitive on paper. In audio, it’s what keeps people with you.

A full example

Here’s a script for one slide in a pre-recorded talk.

Slide headline: “Most delays happened at a single handover point”

Visual: A process diagram with five steps. Step three is highlighted in orange.

Script (in the speaker notes):

Here’s the full process, from request to delivery. Five steps. Most of them ran on time. But look at step three, in orange. That’s the handover between teams. It’s where most of the delays happened. Why? Because nobody owned it. Each team assumed the other one would pick it up.

It moves at a steady pace, pauses before the key point, uses a question to set up the reason, and keeps every word plain.

Quick checklist

  • One idea per sentence
  • Sentence lengths mixed deliberately
  • Full stops placed before key points
  • Jargon swapped for plain words, or defined once
  • Numbers, dates and abbreviations written as spoken
  • Structure signposted with previews, transitions and recaps
  • Each slide opens with a short orienting line
  • Read aloud before you convert
