The Presentation Video Accessibility Checklist

An accessible presentation video is one that more of your students can watch, hear, read and follow, whatever their needs or setting. That includes people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people with low vision, people reading in a second language, and anyone watching on a train with the sound off. The good news is that most of the work happens in the deck, before you export anything.

This checklist walks through the slides, the script, the video settings and the place you publish. Work through it once per course and it quickly becomes habit.

Start with the slides

Accessibility problems in a video usually start as design problems in the deck. Fix them at the source.

Contrast

  • Aim for WCAG AA contrast: at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text and important graphics.
  • Avoid text over busy photos. If you must use a photo, add a solid or semi-opaque panel behind the text.
  • Check your palette in a contrast checker before you build the whole course in it.

For more detail, see contrast and accessibility in slide design.

Colour

  • Never rely on colour alone to carry meaning. If a chart uses red for “below target”, also label it.
  • Use direct labels on chart lines and bars instead of a colour-coded legend.
  • Add a pattern, icon or word where colour is the only difference between two items.

Text size and amount

  • Keep body text at 24pt or larger. Video is often watched on a laptop or phone.
  • One idea per slide. Move the detail into your speaker notes.
  • Use a plain, readable typeface. Avoid long passages in all capitals or italics.

Images and diagrams

  • Every image that carries meaning should be described in the narration. Viewers who can’t see the image still need the point.
  • Decorative images can stay silent, but ask whether they need to be there at all.

Write the narration so it carries the meaning

In a narrated slide video, your speaker notes become the voiceover. That makes the notes your most important accessibility tool. A viewer with low vision relies on them to understand what’s on screen.

Describe what matters

A weak note leans on the visual:

“As you can see here, the trend is pretty clear.”

A stronger note says what the viewer should take away:

“Enrolments rose each year from 2021 to 2024, then dipped slightly in 2025. The dip is the part we’ll look at today.”

The second version works whether or not the viewer can see the chart.

Write for the ear

  • Short sentences. One idea per sentence.
  • Say numbers the way people say them: “about two thousand”, not “2,013”.
  • Spell out abbreviations the first time: “the learning management system, or LMS”.
  • Avoid “this”, “here” and “over there” without saying what they refer to.

Captions

Captions help viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, people watching without sound, and people reading in a second language. They also help everyone follow technical terms.

  • Turn on captions for every course video by default.
  • Check that names, technical terms and acronyms are written in your notes the way you want them to appear.
  • Keep sentences short so each caption fits comfortably on screen.

A note on formats: some platforms prefer a separate caption file (such as SRT or VTT) that viewers can switch on and off. Burned-in captions are part of the picture, so they’re always visible and can’t be restyled. Know what your LMS or institution expects. If a separate caption file is required, you may need to create one with another tool or your platform’s captioning feature. For more on the why and how, see captions on presentation videos.

Pacing and motion

Pacing

  • Give each slide enough time to be read as well as heard. A minimum time per slide helps on slides with little narration.
  • Use a speaking speed that is comfortable rather than brisk. Slightly slower is usually easier to follow, especially for second-language listeners.
  • Pause between sections. A short section-divider slide gives people a moment to catch up.

Motion

  • Animation should reveal points as you discuss them, not decorate.
  • Avoid flashing, rapid flicker and fast strobing effects altogether.
  • Pick calmer transitions, such as a crossfade, for long lectures. Save hard cuts and bold motion for short promotional clips.
  • Large, fast camera moves can be uncomfortable for some viewers. Gentle zoom is fine; constant movement is tiring.

Where and how you publish

  • Give the video a clear title that says what it covers: “Week 3: Measuring reliability”, not “Lecture3_final_v2”.
  • Provide the slides or a text summary alongside the video in your LMS, so learners can read at their own pace or use a screen reader.
  • Use a player with keyboard controls. Most LMS and video platform players support pause and seek from the keyboard.
  • Keep videos short. Split a long lecture into several shorter videos, each on one topic.
  • Tell students what’s available. A one-line note such as “Captions are on by default. Slides are attached below.” helps people know where to look.

Accessible presentation video checklist

Use this as a final pass before you publish.

Area Check
Contrast Text meets 4.5:1 (normal) or 3:1 (large); no text over busy images
Colour Meaning never depends on colour alone; charts are labelled directly
Text Body text 24pt or larger; one idea per slide
Narration Notes describe important visuals and say the point out loud
Language Short sentences; abbreviations explained; numbers said naturally
Captions On, accurate, short lines; caption file provided if your platform requires one
Pacing Comfortable speaking speed; minimum time per slide set
Motion No flashing; calm transitions for long content
Publishing Clear title; slides or summary attached; short, focused videos

Key takeaways

  • Most accessibility work happens in the deck and the notes, not in the edit.
  • Narration should say the point of each visual, not just point at it.
  • Captions help far more people than those who need them for hearing.
  • Calm motion, comfortable pace and short videos make content easier for everyone.
