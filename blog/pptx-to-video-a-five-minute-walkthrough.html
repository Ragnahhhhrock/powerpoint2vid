You have a .pptx file and you want a video. You don’t want to learn an editor, hire a voice artist or set up a microphone. This walkthrough shows how to use a PPTX to video converter that runs in your browser, start to finish, in about the time it takes to make a coffee.

If you want the long version with every option explained, read how to convert PowerPoint to video: the complete guide. This post is the short one.

Before you start: two minutes of prep

A little prep saves a lot of re-exporting. Open your deck and check three things.

  1. It’s a .pptx file. Older .ppt files need to be saved as .pptx first. If your deck is in Google Slides, download it as Microsoft PowerPoint (.pptx). If it’s in Keynote or Canva, export it to .pptx.
  2. Your speaker notes say what you want to hear. The narrator reads the notes. Empty notes mean the slide text gets read instead, or silence, depending on your settings.
  3. Each slide has one idea. A crowded slide is hard to read in a video because viewers can’t pause and squint the way they would at a PDF.

Here’s an example of notes that work well when read aloud:

This is our three-step returns process. First, the customer fills in the online form. Second, we email a prepaid label within one business day. Third, the refund lands as soon as the parcel is scanned at our warehouse.

Short sentences. Clear order. Nothing that sounds odd when spoken.

Step 1: Drop in the file (about 30 seconds)

Open the PowerPoint to video converter and drag your .pptx onto the page, or click to choose it.

The file is read inside your browser. Your slides are parsed, animated, narrated and recorded on your own device. Nothing is uploaded to a server, which matters if your deck has internal numbers, student content or client material.

Step 2: Pick a narration source (about 30 seconds)

Choose what the narrator reads:

  • Speaker notes, else slide text (the default): reads your notes, and falls back to the slide text where a slide has no notes.
  • Speaker notes only: slides without notes stay silent.
  • Slide text only: handy if you never wrote notes.
  • No narration: a silent video, useful for looping displays or social feeds where you’ll rely on captions or on-screen text.

For most decks, the default is right.

Step 3: Choose a voice and speed (about a minute)

Narration uses your browser’s built-in text-to-speech voices. The list depends on your device and browser, so you may see different voices on a work laptop and a home computer.

Play a slide or two in the preview. Try a couple of voices. Adjust the speaking speed so it sounds natural, not rushed. Slightly slower than you think usually works best for training and explainer content.

Step 4: Choose a style and transition (about a minute)

There are six video styles. A quick way to choose:

Style Good for
Clean Training, internal updates, anything that should feel straightforward
Minimal cinematic Brand stories, research summaries, calm explainers
Keynote launch Product announcements and pitch decks
UI springs Software walkthroughs and friendly product content
Kinetic poster Short, punchy social clips
Paper & ink Education, storytelling, anything with a handmade feel

Then pick a transition: crossfade, slide, zoom or none. Crossfade is the safe default. “None” suits fast-moving content.

Step 5: Set the finishing options (about 30 seconds)

A few toggles round out the video:

  • Animate shapes in one by one so points appear as they’re discussed.
  • Burn in captions to add sentence-by-sentence captions to the picture. They’re part of the video itself, so they show up everywhere it plays.
  • Gentle zoom on images adds slow movement to photo slides.
  • Minimum seconds per slide keeps short or silent slides on screen long enough to read. Otherwise timing follows the speech.
  • Record narration into the video. In Chrome, when asked what to share, choose “this tab” and tick “share tab audio” so the voice is captured.

Step 6: Preview, then export (a few minutes, real time)

Watch the preview with the voice on. Listen for words that sound wrong, slides that feel too long, and points that appear before they’re mentioned. Fix them in your deck, reload, and preview again.

When it’s right, export. Exporting needs PowerPoint2Vid Pro. The browser records the video in real time, so a three-minute video takes about three minutes. Keep the tab in the foreground while it records.

You’ll get a WebM file in most browsers, or MP4 in browsers that support it. WebM plays in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and most video editors, and converts to MP4 with common tools if a platform asks for it.

What a PPTX to video converter like this won’t do

It’s worth being clear, so you can plan around it:

  • No AI avatars, stock footage or background music
  • No timeline editing or voice cloning
  • No recording of your own microphone
  • No separate caption files (SRT or VTT); captions are burned in
  • No direct upload to YouTube or an LMS; you upload the file yourself

If you need your own recorded voice, PowerPoint’s built-in video export can include narration and timings you record yourself. It’s a fair option when that’s the goal.

Key takeaways

  • Prep the deck first: .pptx format, clean notes, one idea per slide.
  • Default narration settings suit most decks.
  • Test two or three voices and slow the speed slightly.
  • Match the style to the audience, not your mood.
  • Preview with sound before exporting, every time.
