Slide Masters and Templates: Set Up Once, Save Hours

If you maintain a library of training decks, you’ve probably fixed the same logo, footer or font on dozens of slides by hand. The PowerPoint slide master exists so you don’t have to. Set it up once and every slide that uses it follows the same rules: fonts, colours, logo placement, title position. Change the master, and every slide changes with it.

This post explains what the slide master does, how to set one up sensibly, and why it matters even more when your decks become videos.

What the PowerPoint slide master actually is

Think of the slide master as the rulebook behind your deck. It sits in a separate view (look for Slide Master on the View tab) and controls:

  • Theme fonts for headings and body text.
  • Theme colours used by text, shapes and charts.
  • Background for every slide.
  • Fixed elements such as a logo or footer.
  • Layouts, the individual slide types (title slide, title and content, section header and so on), each with placeholders for text and images.

Your normal slides are built from those layouts. When you type into a placeholder, the text picks up the master’s formatting automatically.

Master, layouts and template: the difference

Term What it is Example
Slide master The top-level rules for the whole deck Brand fonts, colours, logo bottom-right
Layout A slide type that inherits from the master “Title and content”, “Two comparison columns”
Template (.potx) A file that packages the master and layouts for reuse “Company training template” everyone starts from

Why trainers should care

Training content changes often. Policies update, systems change, branding gets refreshed. Without a proper master, each change means touching every slide in every deck.

With a master set up well:

  • Rebrands take minutes. Swap the logo and theme colours once.
  • New decks start consistent. Everyone on the team starts from the same template.
  • Content stays the focus. Authors write content into placeholders instead of fiddling with fonts.
  • Updates are less risky. You’re not hunting for that one slide with the old logo.

How to set up a slide master that works

1. Start with the theme fonts and colours

Set heading and body fonts in the theme, not on individual text boxes. Pick fonts that read well on screen. Choose a colour set with strong contrast between text and background. For accessibility, aim for WCAG AA contrast: at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.

2. Keep the layouts few and useful

Most training decks need only a handful of layouts:

  1. Title slide
  2. Section header
  3. Title and content
  4. Title and image
  5. Two-column comparison
  6. Closing or call-to-action slide

Delete the layouts nobody uses. A short list is easier for authors to choose from.

3. Size text for screens

If your decks will be watched as video, body text should be at least 24pt. Set placeholder sizes in the layouts so authors start at the right size. That also gently discourages long paragraphs.

4. Put fixed elements in the master, not the slides

Logos, footers and slide numbers belong on the master or layouts. If someone has pasted a logo onto 40 individual slides, delete those copies and add it once in the master.

5. Name your layouts clearly

“Layout 7” helps no one. Rename layouts to say what they’re for, such as “Step with screenshot” or “Policy point”.

6. Save it as a template

Save the finished file as a PowerPoint template (.potx) and store it where the whole team can reach it. Ask people to start new decks from the template, not by copying an old deck.

A before-and-after example

Before: An onboarding deck where each slide has a manually placed logo, three different shades of blue, and body text sizes ranging from 14pt to 28pt. A rebrand means opening 60 slides one by one.

After: The same deck rebuilt on a master with six named layouts, one theme colour set and 24pt body text. The rebrand means changing the theme colours and swapping one logo on the master. Every slide updates.

Fixing a deck that ignored the master

You can rescue older decks:

  • Apply the right layout to each slide, then use Reset (on the Home tab) to snap placeholders back to the layout’s formatting.
  • Delete manual text boxes that duplicate placeholders, and move their text into the placeholders.
  • Remove stray logos and footers from individual slides.
  • Check the result in Slide Sorter view for anything that looks out of place.

It takes an hour for a big deck. It saves that hour on every future update.

How a slide master helps when you make videos

When decks become narrated videos, consistency matters even more. Viewers notice when fonts jump or titles shift position between slides, because each slide fills the whole screen.

A clean master also helps converters read your slides properly. Text in real placeholders and shapes is easier to work with than text flattened into images. And for trainers who update content often, a master means the cycle is simple: change the deck, export a new video, replace the old one. Our post on employee onboarding videos walks through that cycle.

Quick checklist

  • Theme fonts and colours set once in the master.
  • Five or six clearly named layouts, the rest deleted.
  • Body text placeholders at 24pt or larger.
  • Logo, footer and slide numbers on the master only.
  • Strong contrast between text and background.
  • Saved as a .potx template the whole team starts from.
