Adding Presentation Videos to Your LMS

You have turned a lecture deck into a narrated video. Now it needs a home where students will actually find it and watch it. A good presentation video LMS setup is less about the upload button and more about the decisions around it: format, hosting, captions, and where the video sits on the page.

This guide walks through those decisions in order. It applies whether your institution runs Moodle, Canvas, Blackboard or something else, because the principles are the same.

Plan the module before you upload

Start with the learning sequence, not the file. A video dropped into a long list of resources is easy to skip. A video placed at the right moment, with a clear job, gets watched.

Ask three questions for each video:

  • What should students be able to do after watching?
  • What comes straight before it, and straight after?
  • How long is it, and is that the right length for this point in the week?

Shorter videos usually work better in an LMS. If your lecture deck runs to 60 slides, split it into several decks of 10 to 15 slides, each covering one idea. Students can pause between them, and you can update one section without touching the rest.

Choose a format your presentation video LMS setup can play

PowerPoint2Vid exports an MP4 video file, with WebM available as an alternative. MP4 plays in nearly every LMS player and institutional video platform, and most video editors open it.

If your platform prefers a different format, convert the file with a common tool before uploading. Check your LMS help pages for accepted formats and file size limits, as these vary between institutions.

A few habits save trouble later:

  • Name files clearly, for example week03-part2-supply-and-demand.mp4.
  • Keep a folder with the source deck next to the exported video.
  • Note the export date in the file name if you update videos each semester.

Upload directly or link from a video platform

You usually have two routes.

Upload into the LMS

This is simple and keeps the video inside your course. It works well for small numbers of short videos. The downside is that large files can count against course storage limits, and the built-in player may be basic.

Host on a video platform and embed

Many universities provide a video platform for teaching content. Others allow unlisted YouTube videos. You upload once, then embed the player in your LMS page. Playback tends to be smoother, and the same video can appear in several courses.

Whichever route you take, follow your institution’s policy. Some content, such as recorded assessments or material involving students, may need to stay inside approved systems.

Captions and transcripts

Captions help students who are deaf or hard of hearing, students reading in a second language, and anyone watching on a train with the sound off.

PowerPoint2Vid can burn captions into the video, sentence by sentence, as the narration plays. Burned-in captions are always visible and travel with the file wherever it goes. They cannot be switched off by the viewer, and there is no separate caption file to upload. If your institution requires a caption file, such as SRT or VTT, you will need to create one with another tool or through your video platform.

A transcript is the other half of accessibility, and you already have one. Your speaker notes are the script. Copy them into a document or an LMS page under the video, lightly tidied, so students can search, skim or print them.

For more on caption choices, see Captions on Presentation Videos: Why and How.

Build the page around the video

Students should know what the video is for before they press play. A short frame around each video does most of the work.

Here is an example layout for one LMS page:

  1. Heading: Week 3, Part 2: Why prices rise when supply falls (6 minutes)
  2. One-line purpose: By the end, you can explain a supply shock using a simple graph.
  3. The video
  4. Try this: Sketch the graph for a drought that halves the wheat harvest. Post it in the forum.
  5. Transcript: Expand to read the full script.

Putting the running time in the heading helps students plan. Putting an activity straight after the video turns watching into doing.

Test it as a student would

Before you open the module, switch to student view and check:

  • The video plays in the browsers your students use.
  • It plays on a phone, not just a laptop.
  • Captions are readable at small sizes.
  • The transcript matches the final narration.
  • Links and activities around the video work.

If the captions look cramped on a phone, the fix is usually in the deck. Fewer words per slide and larger text leave more room for captions at the bottom of the frame.

Keeping videos current

Course content changes. A date moves, a policy updates, a reading is replaced. With slide-based video, you edit the deck and export again. There is no re-recording session and no need to match your voice to the old take.

Keep a short log for each course: video name, source deck, last export date, and what changed. When the next semester starts, you can see at a glance which videos need a refresh.

For a broader view of the lecture-to-video workflow, see Lecturers: Turn Lecture Slides into Course Videos.

Quick checklist

  • Split long lectures into short, single-topic videos.
  • Check your LMS or video platform for accepted formats and size limits.
  • Export MP4, and convert the file if your platform needs another format.
  • Decide between direct upload and embedding from a video platform.
  • Burn in captions, and check whether your institution also needs caption files.
  • Publish the speaker notes as a transcript.
  • Frame each video with a heading, purpose, running time and activity.
  • Test in student view on a phone.
