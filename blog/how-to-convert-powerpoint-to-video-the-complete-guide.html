You have a slide deck that already explains something well. Now you need it to work without you in the room. The simplest answer is to convert PowerPoint to video: the slides play in order, a voice explains each one, and anyone can watch it whenever suits them. This guide covers the whole job, from getting the deck ready to sharing the finished file.

It’s written for anyone with slides and a reason to send them: lecturers, trainers, marketers, salespeople and students. Read it top to bottom the first time, then use the checklist near the end each time you make a new video.

Why turn slides into a video at all?

A deck on its own relies on a presenter. Send it as a PDF and the reader gets the headlines but misses the explanation. A video carries both.

  • It explains itself. The narration fills in what the slides leave out.
  • People watch when it suits them. No meeting to book, no time zones to juggle.
  • Everyone hears the same message. The tenth viewer gets exactly what the first one did.
  • It travels. A video file works on a website, in a learning management system, on YouTube or LinkedIn, or as a link in an email.
  • It’s easy to update. Change the deck, make the video again.

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer’s work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. A narrated deck is a straightforward way to give them both.

Three ways to convert PowerPoint to video

There’s no single right method. Each of these has a place.

1. PowerPoint’s own video export

PowerPoint can export a presentation as a video file. Before exporting, you can record slide timings and your own narration as you click through. If you’re comfortable recording your voice and you have a quiet room and a decent microphone, this works well. Look for the Export option in PowerPoint and choose the video format.

2. Screen recording

You can present the deck in slideshow mode and record your screen and microphone. It’s quick for a one-off, but every mistake means another take.

3. A dedicated converter

A converter takes the .pptx file and builds the video for you. The best ones do more than record your slides. PowerPoint2Vid, for example, uses your speaker notes as the narration and upgrades the deck itself, turning each slide into an animated scene. You don’t need to record your own voice, and when the deck changes you simply make the video again.

Before you start: get the deck ready for video

A deck built to be presented live often needs a little tidying before it works as a video. Ten minutes here saves a lot of re-exporting later.

  • One idea per slide. In a video, each slide stays up while its narration plays. A slide with three ideas becomes a long, static shot.
  • Make the headline the point. “Returns are processed within five days” says more than “Returns”.
  • Bigger text, less of it. Aim for body text of 24pt or more. Move detail into the speaker notes, where it becomes narration.
  • Use a 16:9 layout. Most screens are widescreen, and so is most video.
  • Check contrast. WCAG AA asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Light grey on white won’t survive video compression or a phone screen.
  • Remove anything sensitive. Hidden comments, old draft slides and internal notes have a way of ending up in front of the wrong audience.

Write speaker notes for the ear

If your narration is generated from your notes, your speaker notes are your script. Notes written as reminders to yourself don’t sound good read aloud. Notes written as spoken sentences do.

Keep sentences short. Use the words you’d say, not the words you’d write in a report. Say numbers the way people say them. Spell out abbreviations the first time.

Here’s a before and after for an onboarding slide.

Before (notes as reminders): “Day 1 - laptop, accts (email, HRIS), buddy intro. Mention IT ticket if issues.”

After (notes as a script): “On your first day, three things happen. You’ll get your laptop. You’ll get logins for email and the HR system. And you’ll meet your buddy, who is your go-to person for the first month. If anything doesn’t work, log a ticket with IT and they’ll sort it out.”

The second version is longer, but it’s much easier to follow by ear.

Captions and accessibility

Captions help people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people watching in a second language and anyone watching with the sound off. PowerPoint2Vid includes captions in the video, so they’re always visible, on every platform, with no extra file to manage.

Common problems and quick fixes

  • A word is mispronounced. Rewrite it in the notes the way it sounds, or rephrase the sentence.
  • The voice feels too fast or too slow. Change the speaking speed and make the video again.
  • A slide feels thin. Add a sentence to its notes so the narration has something to say.
  • The video feels flat. Check each slide makes one clear point. The tighter the story in the deck, the stronger the finished scenes.
  • The file won’t open. Check it’s a .pptx, not a .ppt, .key or PDF.
  • A number looks wrong. Check the figure in the original deck and the speaker note, fix it there and make the video again.

Quick checklist

  1. One idea per slide, with headlines that make a point.
  2. Body text 24pt or larger, with good contrast.
  3. Speaker notes written as spoken sentences.
  4. Deck saved as .pptx.
  5. Voice and speed chosen.
  6. Story checked: one clear point per slide, in an order that builds.
  7. Finished MP4 downloaded and watched all the way through.
