How Much Text Is Too Much on a Slide?

By Mal Gordon · 14 November 2026 · 5 min read

Most people know the feeling. A slide comes up, it is full of words, and you stop listening to the speaker so you can read. Then you fall behind on both. Too much text on slides is one of the most common presentation problems, and it gets worse when the deck becomes a video, because the viewer cannot ask you to slow down.

There is no single word count that works for every slide. But there are simple tests that tell you when a slide is overloaded, and a reliable way to fix it.

Why too much text on slides hurts

When people see words and hear different words at the same time, they try to process both and do neither well. Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer’s work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone, and that reading on-screen text while hearing the same thing spoken can get in the way.

In practice, a text-heavy slide causes three problems:

  • Attention splits. The audience reads ahead while you talk.
  • Nothing stands out. When everything is written down, nothing looks important.
  • Text gets small. More words means smaller fonts, which are hard to read on a laptop or phone.

In a video, there is a fourth problem. The slide is only on screen for as long as the narration runs. If the viewer cannot read it in that time, they have to pause or give up.

Five tests for an overloaded slide

Run each slide through these quick checks.

1. The glance test

Look at the slide for three seconds, then look away. Can you say what it was about? If not, it is doing too much.

2. The headline test

Read only the headline. Does it make a point on its own? If the headline is a label like “Background” and the point is buried in bullet four, the slide needs restructuring.

3. The font size test

If the body text has shrunk below about 24pt to fit, there is too much of it. For slides that will become video, treat 24pt as a floor.

4. The read-aloud test

If you could read the slide aloud word for word as your script, the slide is carrying the script. Move it.

5. The two-ideas test

If the slide needs two headlines to describe it, it is two slides. Split it.

Where the extra text should go

The words you cut do not disappear. They move to where they help.

  • Speaker notes. The detail, the explanation and the examples belong here. When the deck becomes a narrated video, the notes become the voiceover.
  • A handout or follow-up document. Reference material, full tables and fine print people need to keep.
  • More slides. One crowded slide often becomes two or three clear ones. More slides with less on each is usually easier to follow.

A before and after example

Here is a typical overloaded slide from a training deck.

Before

Headline: Leave policy

  • Annual leave accrues at four weeks per year for full-time employees, pro rata for part-time
  • Requests must be submitted at least two weeks in advance via the HR portal
  • Managers will respond within five working days
  • Leave over eight weeks may need to be taken by agreement
  • Personal leave is separate and covered in section 4

After

Headline: Request leave two weeks ahead, through the HR portal

On the slide: a simple three-step graphic. Submit request, manager reviews, you get a reply.

Speaker notes:

“Full-time staff build up four weeks of annual leave a year. Part-time staff build up the same, pro rata. To take leave, send a request through the HR portal at least two weeks before. Your manager will reply within five working days. Personal leave works differently, and we’ll cover it next.”

The slide now makes one point. The narration carries the detail. The rule about leave over eight weeks can go on its own slide or in the policy document.

A rough guide to text on a slide

There is no hard limit, but this gives you a starting point:

Slide element Aim for
Headline One sentence that makes a point
Bullet points Three or fewer, if any
Words per bullet A short phrase, not a full sentence
Body text size 24pt or larger for video
Ideas per slide One

Some slides need more, such as a definition the audience must read exactly, or a quote. That is fine. Give those slides more time on screen and keep the narration close to the words shown.

When more text is acceptable

There are fair exceptions.

  • Reference decks that are read, not presented, can carry more text. Just do not turn them into a video without trimming.
  • Exact wording such as a legal statement or a quote should be shown in full.
  • Language learners may benefit from key terms on screen alongside narration.

Even then, keep the font large and give the viewer time to read.

Quick checklist

  • Check each slide with the glance test
  • Make every headline a point, not a label
  • Keep body text 24pt or larger
  • Move explanations into the speaker notes
  • Split any slide that holds two ideas
  • Put reference detail in a handout, not on the slide
  • Give text-heavy exceptions extra time on screen
