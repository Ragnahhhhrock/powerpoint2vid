Mac users have more options than they might think. Whether you work in PowerPoint for Mac, Keynote or just have a .pptx someone emailed you, you can convert PowerPoint to video on Mac computers without special hardware or editing software. This guide covers the three main routes, when each one makes sense, and how to sort out the snags Mac users tend to hit.

If you want the full background on converting decks, start with our [complete guide to converting PowerPoint to video](/blog/how-to-convert-powerpoint-to-video-the-complete-guide). This post focuses on what's specific to the Mac.

## Three ways to convert PowerPoint to video on Mac

| Route | Narration | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| PowerPoint for Mac's own export | Your own recorded voice and timings, if you record them | Decks where you want to speak the narration yourself |
| Keynote export | Your own recorded voice, if you record it in Keynote | People who already work in Keynote |
| A browser-based converter | Text-to-speech from your speaker notes | Narrated videos without recording your voice |

Each route is fair game. Pick the one that matches how you want the narration to sound and how much time you have.

## Route 1: PowerPoint for Mac's built-in export

Recent versions of PowerPoint for Mac can export a presentation as a video file. Look for the Export option in the File menu and choose a video format.

Before exporting, you can record slide timings and your own narration with PowerPoint's recording features. The export then uses those timings and that audio.

**Good for:** people who are comfortable recording their own voice and want PowerPoint's own animations and transitions in the video.

**Keep in mind:**

- You need a quiet room and a decent microphone for good narration.
- If you change a slide later, you may need to re-record the narration for that slide.
- Exports of long decks can take a while.

## Route 2: Export from Keynote

If you open the .pptx in Keynote, or you built the deck there in the first place, Keynote can export a presentation as a movie. Look for the Export option in the File menu.

Keynote also lets you record a voiceover while you click through the slides, which it can include in the export.

**Good for:** Keynote users who want Keynote's own transitions and builds.

**Keep in mind:**

- PowerPoint files opened in Keynote can shift slightly: fonts may be substituted and some animations may change. Check each slide before exporting.
- As with PowerPoint, narration means recording yourself.

## Route 3: A browser-based converter

A browser converter works the same on a Mac as on any other computer, because it runs in the browser. PowerPoint2Vid is one example. It reads your .pptx, uses your speaker notes as the script, and narrates them with the browser's built-in text-to-speech voices. You don't need PowerPoint or Keynote installed at all.

**Good for:** people who don't want to record their own voice, or who update decks often and want to re-export without re-recording.

### Step by step on a Mac

1. **Get a .pptx file.** If you're in Keynote, export the presentation as a PowerPoint file first. If you have an old .ppt file, open it and save it as .pptx.
2. **Write or tidy your speaker notes.** These become the narration. Short, spoken sentences work best.
3. **Open the converter in your browser** and drop in the .pptx.
4. **Choose a narration script.** Speaker notes, slide text, a mix (notes where they exist, otherwise slide text), or no narration.
5. **Pick a voice and speed.** The voices available depend on your Mac and browser. macOS includes a range of system voices, so try a few.
6. **Choose a style and transition.** Preview with the voice to check pacing.
7. **Export.** The browser records in real time, so keep the tab in the foreground until it finishes.

### A sample speaker note

Here's what good notes look like for a single slide headed "Orders now ship within 24 hours":

> Here's the big change. Orders placed before two pm now ship the same day. Everything else ships within twenty-four hours. That's a full day faster than last year.

Notice the numbers are written the way you'd say them. That helps text-to-speech voices read them naturally.

## Mac-specific snags and how to fix them

### The video won't open in QuickTime

Browser converters often export WebM, which plays in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and most video editors but may not open in QuickTime Player or some other Mac apps. Open it in a browser to watch it, or convert it to MP4 with a common video tool. Some browsers can export MP4 directly. Our post on [MP4 or WebM](/blog/mp4-or-webm-choosing-a-format-for-your-presentation-video) explains the difference.

### Narration is missing from the video

In Chrome, if you want the narration recorded into the video, choose "this tab" when asked what to share and tick "share tab audio". If you skip that, the video can end up silent.

### Fonts look different

If your deck uses fonts that aren't installed on your Mac, they may be replaced. Install the fonts, or switch to fonts available on your system before converting.

### The voice sounds robotic

Try other voices. macOS offers several, and some sound noticeably more natural than others. Slowing the speaking speed slightly also helps.

### The export stalls

Real-time recording needs the tab to stay visible. Don't minimise the window or switch to another tab during export, and keep your Mac from sleeping.

## Quick checklist

- A .pptx file (export from Keynote or save old .ppt files first).
- Speaker notes written for the ear.
- Fonts installed or swapped for system fonts.
- A voice and speed chosen after previewing.
- "Share tab audio" ticked in Chrome if you're recording narration.
- The tab kept in the foreground during export.
- A plan for WebM files if you need MP4.
