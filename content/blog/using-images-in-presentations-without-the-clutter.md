Good images in presentations do one job: they make the point land faster than words can. Bad ones fill space. Most decks have too many pictures, too small, placed wherever there was room. The fix is not more design talent. It is a few simple rules about choosing, cropping and placing.

## Why images in presentations often backfire

An image competes with your words for attention. When it supports the message, the viewer gets the idea twice, once through the picture and once through the narration. When it doesn't, the viewer has to work out why it is there.

Common ways images go wrong:

- **Decoration.** A stock photo of people shaking hands on a slide about partnerships adds nothing the headline didn't already say.
- **Too many at once.** Four small photos in a grid force the eye to scan, and none of them gets enough size to matter.
- **Text on top of busy backgrounds.** A headline over a detailed photo becomes hard to read, especially once a video is compressed.
- **Low resolution.** A logo pulled from an email signature looks soft and blurry at full screen.
- **Inconsistent style.** A flat illustration next to a glossy photo next to a hand-drawn icon makes the deck feel stitched together.

## Ask one question before adding any image

"What would the viewer lose if this picture were gone?"

If the answer is "nothing", leave it out. If the answer is "they wouldn't see what the product looks like" or "they wouldn't understand the size of the site", keep it and give it room.

Images that usually earn their place:

- A screenshot of the actual product, cropped to the part you are talking about.
- A photo of the real place, team or object, not a generic stand-in.
- A diagram that shows how parts connect.
- A before-and-after pair that makes a change visible.

## Choose fewer, bigger pictures

One strong image at full width beats three small ones. On a 16:9 screen, a picture that fills the slide reads instantly. A thumbnail in the corner reads as clutter.

If you have three photos you love, consider three slides. Each one gets the full frame, and each gets its own sentence of narration. This fits the idea behind [the one idea per slide rule](/blog/the-one-idea-per-slide-rule-and-when-to-break-it): if the images say different things, they belong on different slides.

### Crop with purpose

Cropping is the fastest way to improve an image. Cut away anything that doesn't support the point.

- Crop screenshots to the button, field or chart you are talking about.
- Crop people photos so faces sit roughly on the upper third line.
- Crop wide landscapes to a strong horizon or a single subject.

Most slide tools let you crop directly on the slide. Keep the original file somewhere safe in case you need to re-crop later.

## Placing images so text stays readable

There are three reliable layouts. Pick one per slide.

| Layout | When to use it | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Full-bleed image, short headline | Mood, place, a single striking moment | Put text over a calm area, or add a dark band behind it |
| Split screen (image one side, text the other) | Screenshots, product shots, people | Keep the text side to a headline and two short lines |
| Image with a caption underneath | Diagrams, charts, evidence | Make the caption a full sentence that states the point |

### Keep text off busy areas

If you must place a headline over a photo, find the quiet part of the image: sky, a plain wall, a blurred background. If there is no quiet part, add a semi-opaque dark or light band behind the text. Check the contrast. WCAG AA asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, and those ratios are a sensible target for slides too.

## A before-and-after example

Here is a slide from a small-business product deck.

**Before**

- Headline: "Our new range"
- Four product photos in a grid, each about the size of a postage stamp
- A bulleted list of eight features underneath
- The company logo in two corners

**After**

- Headline: "The Trail Pack carries a full day's gear at under a kilo"
- One large photo of the pack, cropped tight, on the left half of the slide
- Two short lines on the right: "Under 1 kg" and "Fits a 15-inch laptop"
- The other three products moved to their own slides
- Logo once, on the title slide only

Speaker note for the new slide:

> This is the Trail Pack. It weighs under a kilo, and it still fits a fifteen-inch laptop. Most packs this light make you choose between the two.

The slide now has one image, one claim and one sentence of support. The narration does the rest.

## Keep a consistent visual style

Pick a lane and stay in it for the whole deck:

- **Photos only**, ideally with similar lighting and colour temperature.
- **Illustrations only**, from one set or one illustrator.
- **Screenshots and diagrams**, with consistent borders or shadows.

Mixing styles is fine when it's a decision, not an accident.

## Check image quality and rights

Before you build the final deck:

1. **Resolution.** Use images large enough to fill the frame without looking soft. If a photo looks fuzzy at full screen in slideshow mode, it will look fuzzy in the video too.
2. **File size.** Very large images can slow down your slide software. Most tools offer an option to compress pictures in the file.
3. **Licensing.** Use your own photos, images you have paid for, or images clearly licensed for reuse in commercial work. Keep a note of where each one came from.
4. **Alt text.** Add a short description to each meaningful image. It helps anyone using a screen reader with the original deck and forces you to articulate what the picture is for.

## How images behave in a slide video

When a deck becomes a video, images get more screen time and more attention than in a live talk. A few things to keep in mind:

- **Motion helps static photos.** A slow zoom on an image keeps a long narrated slide from feeling frozen. PowerPoint2Vid has a "gentle zoom on images" option for exactly this.
- **Builds can reveal images in order.** With "animate shapes in one by one", a photo can appear just as the narration mentions it.
- **Captions need clear space.** If you plan to burn in captions, leave the bottom of the slide calm so caption text doesn't fight the picture.

## Quick checklist

- Every image answers "what would the viewer lose without it?"
- One main image per slide, shown large.
- Cropped to the point, with nothing extra in frame.
- Text sits on a calm area or a contrast band.
- One consistent visual style across the deck.
- High enough resolution for full-screen video.
- Licensed for your use, with alt text added.
