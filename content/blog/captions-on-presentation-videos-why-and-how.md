If you're turning lecture slides or training decks into video, captions for presentation video aren't an optional extra. They decide whether some of your audience can follow at all. This post covers who captions help, the two main types, and how to write slides and notes that caption cleanly.

## Who captions help

It's easy to think of captions as something for a small group. In practice they help a wide range of viewers.

- **People who are deaf or hard of hearing.** Without captions, the narration is simply missing.
- **People studying or working in a second language.** Reading along makes unfamiliar accents and technical terms easier to follow.
- **People watching with the sound off.** On a train, in a shared office, in a library, or with a sleeping baby nearby.
- **People who process information better by reading.** Some viewers like to see and hear the same words.
- **Anyone revising.** Captions make it easier to scan back to the point you missed.

For lecturers, captions also tend to come up in accessibility policies and course reviews. Check what your institution expects before you publish.

## Burned-in captions vs closed captions

There are two main ways to put captions on a video. They suit different situations.

| | Burned-in (open) captions | Closed captions |
|---|---|---|
| How they work | Part of the video image | A separate text track, often an SRT or VTT file |
| Viewer can turn them off | No | Yes |
| Works on every player | Yes | Only where the player supports caption tracks |
| Editable after export | No, re-export the video | Yes, edit the text file |
| Searchable by platforms | No | Often yes |

### When burned-in captions make sense

Burned-in captions are always there. That's useful when you don't control where the video ends up: social feeds, email attachments, a shared drive or a player that ignores caption files. They're also simpler, because there's only one file to manage.

### When closed captions make sense

Closed captions let each viewer choose. Some learning management systems and video platforms expect a caption track, and some accessibility policies require one. If that's your situation, you'll need a caption file, which most video platforms can help you create.

### Using both

Many people burn captions in for social and email, and upload a separate caption track for the version that lives on YouTube or the LMS. Your speaker notes are a ready-made transcript for that track.

## How to get good captions for presentation video

Captions are only as good as the words behind them. If your narration comes from speaker notes, you control both.

### Write notes in short sentences

Long sentences become long captions that cover the slide or flick past too fast. Aim for sentences that fit comfortably on one or two lines.

**Before:** "The assessment for this unit, which is worth forty per cent of your final grade and is due at the end of week eight, involves a written report and a short presentation."

**After:** "This unit has one major assessment. It's worth forty per cent of your grade. It's due at the end of week eight. You'll write a report and give a short presentation."

The second version reads better as captions and sounds better spoken.

### Spell things the way you want them shown

The captions show what's in your script. If your notes say "approx" or "e.g.", that's what appears on screen, and it's not what the voice says. Write words out in full.

### Leave room at the bottom of the slide

Captions usually sit near the bottom of the frame. If your slide has key text or a chart axis there, the captions may cover it. Keep the lower part of each slide light, or move important content higher.

### Check proper nouns and jargon

Names, course codes and technical terms are where captions and narration most often go wrong. Read through the slides with the captions on and fix anything that looks or sounds off.

## Captions and slide design

Captions add a layer of text to every frame, so slide design matters more.

- **Less text on the slide.** Viewers can't read a dense slide and the captions at the same time.
- **Strong contrast.** WCAG AA asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text. Make sure both the slide text and the caption area meet it.
- **Don't rely on colour alone.** If a chart uses red and green to show good and bad, add labels too.

## Quick checklist

1. Decide where the video will live and whether you need burned-in captions, a caption file or both.
2. Rewrite long sentences in your notes into short ones.
3. Write out abbreviations and numbers the way they should appear.
4. Keep the bottom of each slide clear.
5. Check names and technical terms with captions turned on.
6. Keep your notes as a transcript for any closed caption track.
