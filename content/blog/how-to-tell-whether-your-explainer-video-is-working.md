You made an explainer video, put it on your site and shared it around. Now what? Views alone tell you very little. The explainer video metrics that matter are the ones linked to the job you gave the video in the first place. Get that link right and you'll know whether to keep it, fix it or replace it.

This post walks through how to pick a goal, which numbers to watch, how to read them, and what to change when the numbers are weak.

## Start with the job, not the numbers

Before you open any analytics, write one sentence: "This video exists to..."

Some common answers:

- "...help visitors understand what we do, so more of them book a demo."
- "...answer the pricing question before people email us."
- "...get people to sign up for the waitlist after the launch post."
- "...explain the setup steps so new customers don't need a call."

That sentence decides your main metric. A video meant to drive demo bookings should be judged on bookings, not likes. A video meant to answer a question should be judged on whether the question stops coming in.

## The explainer video metrics worth tracking

Most video platforms and website analytics tools report some version of the measures below. Names vary, but the ideas are the same.

### Attention metrics

- **Plays or play rate.** Of the people who saw the video, how many pressed play? A low play rate usually points to placement, thumbnail or title, not the video itself.
- **Average watch time.** How long people watched, on average. Compare it with the video length.
- **Audience retention.** A curve showing what share of viewers are still watching at each moment. This is the most useful chart you have.

### Action metrics

- **Click-through on the call to action.** If the video ends with "Book a demo", how many viewers clicked?
- **Conversions on the page.** Sign-ups, enquiries or purchases from visitors who watched, compared with those who didn't.
- **Assisted conversions.** People who watched, left, then came back and converted later.

### Outcome metrics

- **Fewer repeat questions.** For help videos, a drop in the emails or tickets on that topic.
- **Shorter sales conversations.** Prospects arriving already knowing the basics.
- **Quality of leads.** Enquiries that mention the video or ask more specific questions.

## How to read the retention curve

The retention curve shows exactly where your video loses people. A few common shapes:

- **Steep drop in the first few seconds.** The opening doesn't match what people expected, or it's too slow. Lead with the problem or the payoff.
- **Steady, gentle decline.** Normal. Most videos lose viewers gradually.
- **A sudden cliff partway through.** Something on that slide made people leave. Look at it closely. Is it a dense chart, a long pause, or a section that feels off-topic?
- **A bump upward.** People are rewinding to rewatch a section. That part may be confusing, or very useful. Either way, it's worth a look.

Write down the timestamp of any cliff, then find the slide it matches in your deck.

## A worked example

Say a small accounting software company puts a two-minute explainer on its pricing page. The job: "help visitors understand the plans so they start a trial instead of emailing sales."

After a month, they look at three things:

1. **Play rate** on the pricing page is modest. Many visitors scroll past.
2. **Retention** is strong for the first minute, then drops sharply at the slide comparing all four plans in a dense table.
3. **Emails asking "which plan is right for me?"** haven't changed much.

What they change:

- Move the video higher on the page and give it a clear label: "Which plan fits you? (2 min)".
- Replace the dense table slide with three slides, one per customer type. New headline: "Sole traders: the Starter plan covers everything you need."
- Rewrite the notes for those slides to name the customer type first.

A month later they compare the same three measures. That's the whole loop: one goal, a few numbers, one round of changes, check again.

## Set a baseline before you change anything

You can't tell if a change worked without a "before". Before you edit the video or its placement:

- Note the current play rate, average watch time and main conversion number.
- Note the time period (for example, the last four weeks).
- Write down what you're changing and when.

Then compare the same period after the change. Keep other changes to the page to a minimum while you test, or you won't know what made the difference.

## Common reasons an explainer underperforms

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Few people press play | Poor placement or weak thumbnail | Move it higher; add a clear label with the running time |
| Big drop in the first seconds | Slow or vague opening | Start with the viewer's problem in one sentence |
| Cliff mid-video | One confusing or dense slide | Split the slide; simplify the visual |
| Good watch time, few actions | Weak or missing call to action | End with one clear next step and a link near the video |
| Lots of views, same questions | Video doesn't answer the real question | Rewrite around the exact words customers use |

## Don't over-measure

Small businesses rarely need a complex dashboard. One main metric, two supporting ones, checked monthly, is plenty. More numbers often mean less action.

A simple monthly routine:

1. Check your main metric against last month.
2. Look at the retention curve for any new cliffs.
3. Pick one thing to change.
4. Write it down with the date.

## Key takeaways

- Decide the video's job first. That decides the metric.
- Use the retention curve to find the exact slide that loses people.
- Measure a baseline, change one thing, then compare.
- Judge the video by outcomes like enquiries, sign-ups and fewer repeat questions, not views alone.
