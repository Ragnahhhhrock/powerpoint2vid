Bullet points are not the enemy. They are fast to write and easy to edit. The trouble is that a slide of six bullets asks the audience to read while you talk, and most people cannot do both. Looking for alternatives to bullet points is really about finding the shape of your content and showing that shape instead of a list.

This post gives you a quick way to diagnose a bullet slide, six formats to try instead, and a before and after rewrite.

## Why bullet slides fall flat

A bullet list makes every point look equally important. It hides the relationship between ideas. Is point three a result of point two? Is point five the most important? A list cannot say.

Bullets also tempt you to put the whole script on the slide. When that slide becomes a video, the narration repeats the text word for word, and the viewer reads ahead and tunes out.

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone, and that narration plus a visual tends to work better than narration plus the same words on screen. That is a strong case for moving the words into the speaker notes and giving the slide a visual job.

## Diagnose the list first

Read your bullets and ask what connects them.

- **They happen in order?** It is a process.
- **They happen over time?** It is a timeline.
- **They contrast two things?** It is a comparison.
- **One matters far more than the rest?** It is a single statement.
- **They are parts of a whole?** It is a group or a diagram.
- **They are numbers?** It is a chart.

Once you know the shape, the visual follows.

## Six alternatives to bullet points

### 1. The single statement

Pick the most important bullet and make it the whole slide. Large text, plenty of space. The rest goes into the speaker notes or onto the next slides.

### 2. The process

Turn ordered steps into boxes joined by arrows. Three to five steps fit comfortably. Label each box with two to four words.

### 3. The timeline

Put dates along a horizontal line with a short label at each point. This works well for project plans, history, and "what happens next".

### 4. The comparison

Two columns, two headings, matched rows. Before and after, option A and option B, old policy and new policy. Make the side you recommend stand out with colour.

### 5. The grid of icons

For three to six parallel items, use a grid with an icon and a short label for each. Keep icon style and size consistent. Always include the word, not just the picture.

### 6. The chart

If your bullets contain numbers, they probably want to be a chart. Highlight the one value that matters and grey out the rest.

## A before and after rewrite

**Before**

Title: Q3 update

- Revenue up on last quarter
- New customers mostly from referral program
- Churn slightly lower
- Support response times improved
- Hiring two more support staff in Q4

**After**

This one slide becomes two.

Slide 1 title: Referrals drove most of our new customers this quarter

Visual: a simple bar chart of new customers by channel, with the referral bar in the accent colour and the others grey.

Slide 2 title: Faster support, and two more hires to keep it that way

Visual: a two-column comparison. Left: "Q2 response time". Right: "Q3 response time". Below, a small note: "+2 support staff in Q4".

Sample speaker note for slide 1:

"Most of our new customers this quarter came through referrals. That's the referral program we launched in July doing its job. Paid ads and events also contributed, but referrals were well ahead."

Each slide now makes one point, the headline says what that point is, and the narration adds the detail. For more on headlines like these, see [Slide Headlines That Make a Point](/blog/slide-headlines-that-make-a-point).

## When bullets are fine

Some content really is a list.

- An agenda.
- A checklist people will refer back to.
- A short set of requirements.

Even then, keep it short. Three to five items, no full sentences, and text large enough to read on a phone. If you need more, split the slide. For a closer look at limits, see [How Much Text Is Too Much on a Slide?](/blog/how-much-text-is-too-much-on-a-slide)

## Making visuals work in video

Visual slides get better when narrated. Each part of a process or comparison can appear as the voice reaches it.

- Write speaker notes that describe the visual in order, left to right or top to bottom.
- Use the "animate shapes in one by one" option so parts of a diagram arrive in sequence.
- Keep labels short so burned-in captions have room at the bottom of the frame.
- Preview with voice and check each reveal lines up with the narration.

## Quick checklist

- Read each bullet slide and name its shape: process, timeline, comparison, statement, group or chart.
- Rebuild the slide in that shape.
- Write a headline that states the point.
- Move detail into the speaker notes.
- Keep any remaining lists to three to five short items.
