"How many slides should I have?" is one of the most common questions presenters ask. You will find plenty of rules online, often with a fixed number per minute. The trouble is that a fixed rule ignores what is on the slides. The honest answer to how many slides a presentation needs is: as many as your ideas need, and no more.

That sounds vague, so this post gives you a practical way to work it out.

## Why fixed rules fall short

Slide-count rules try to stop two problems: too many slides rushed through, and too few slides crammed with text. Both are real. But the number itself is not the cause.

- A 10-minute talk with 30 simple slides, each showing one image and a headline, can feel calm and clear.
- A 10-minute talk with 5 slides, each holding a dense table, can feel overwhelming.

What matters is **how much each slide asks of the audience** and **how long you spend on it**. Count ideas, not slides.

## How many slides for a presentation? Start with ideas

Use this method instead.

### Step 1: List your ideas
Write down every point you need to make, one per line. Not slide titles, but complete statements.

- "Our sign-ups grew steadily this year."
- "Most new users come from referrals."
- "Referral users stay longer than ad users."
- "We should invest in the referral program."

### Step 2: Give each idea one slide
One idea per slide is the starting point. If a point needs a chart and an example, that might be two slides. If two points are tightly linked, they might share one.

### Step 3: Add the structural slides
Most presentations also need:
- A title slide
- An agenda or "what you'll learn" slide (for longer talks)
- Section dividers (for talks with distinct parts)
- A conclusion or call to action
- References or contact details if needed

### Step 4: Check against your time
Now compare the total to your time limit. A rough working range for many presenters is about 30 seconds to two minutes per slide, depending on how much each one carries. If your count looks too high for your time, you probably have too many ideas, not too many slides. Cut ideas.

## Time per slide depends on the slide

Different slides need different amounts of time.

| Slide type | Typical time on screen |
|---|---|
| Title or section divider | A few seconds |
| Single image with a headline | Short |
| Key point with a short explanation | Moderate |
| Chart with one clear message | Moderate to longer |
| Complex diagram built up in steps | Longer, or split across slides |

Rather than aiming for an average, give each slide the time its content needs.

## Signs you have too many slides

- Several slides make the same point in slightly different ways.
- You find yourself skipping slides when you rehearse.
- Slides exist "just in case" someone asks.
- The talk runs over every time you practise.

**Fix:** merge repeats, move "just in case" slides to an appendix, and cut ideas that do not support your main message.

## Signs you have too few slides

- Slides are crowded with bullets and small text.
- You stay on one slide for several minutes while talking through many points.
- The audience reads ahead of you.
- Charts try to show three messages at once.

**Fix:** split dense slides. One heavy slide often becomes three light ones, which is easier to follow and actually feels faster.

## An example: one dense slide becomes three

**Before (one slide):**
Headline: "Customer feedback summary"
Bullets: setup was slow; support was helpful; pricing page confusing; mobile app praised; reporting needs more filters; onboarding emails useful.

**After (three slides):**
1. "Customers like our people and our app" with two quotes or icons.
2. "Setup and pricing cause the most friction" with a simple visual.
3. "Reporting is the top feature request" with a single example.

Same content, same time, but each slide now has one message the audience can grasp at a glance.

## Slide counts in video

In a narrated video, slide count matters a little differently. Viewers cannot ask you to slow down, so they need:

- Enough slides that the screen changes as the story moves.
- Not so many that slides flash past before they can read the headline.

## Key takeaways

- There is no magic number. Count ideas, not slides.
- Start with one idea per slide, then add structural slides.
- Check the total against your time and cut ideas if it does not fit.
- Merge repeated slides; split crowded ones.
- In video, the narration sets the pace, so keep notes short and focused.
