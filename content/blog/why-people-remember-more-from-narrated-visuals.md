Think about the last time you understood something difficult. Odds are someone showed you a picture while they explained it. A diagram on a whiteboard. A sketch on a napkin. A chart with someone pointing at the important part. That pairing of words and visuals is the core idea behind explainer video learning, and it's one of the better-supported ideas in education research.

This post explains why narrated visuals work, what can get in the way, and how to design slide videos that help people learn rather than just watch.

## The basic idea: two channels are better than one

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. The reasoning is fairly intuitive.

We take in information through more than one channel. Roughly speaking, there's a channel for what we see and a channel for what we hear. Each has limited capacity. When you give someone a page of text, everything goes through the visual channel. When you show a picture and explain it out loud, the load is shared. The picture goes through the eyes. The explanation goes through the ears. The learner connects the two.

That connection is where understanding happens. A labelled diagram plus a spoken explanation gives the learner two linked ways into the same idea.

## Why explainer video learning beats reading slides alone

A slide deck shared as a PDF puts a lot of work on the reader. They have to read the text, look at the visual, and work out how they relate, all through their eyes at once. Dense slides make this worse.

A narrated version changes that:

- **The visual stays visual.** The slide can show a diagram or chart without a paragraph explaining it.
- **The explanation moves to audio.** The narration does the explaining, in the order the learner needs it.
- **Timing is guided.** The voice tells the viewer where to look and when.
- **Attention stays together.** Words and pictures arrive at the same moment, so the learner doesn't have to hold one in mind while searching for the other.

## What gets in the way of learning

Narrated visuals aren't automatically good. A few common habits work against them.

### Reading the slide text aloud word for word

If the slide has five bullet points and the narration reads them out, the learner gets the same words twice, through both channels at once. That tends to compete rather than help. It's better to put a short phrase or visual on the slide and let the narration expand on it.

### Decorative extras

Background music, unrelated stock images and busy animations all take attention without adding meaning. Cutting extraneous material is one of the more consistent recommendations in multimedia learning research. If something doesn't help explain the idea, it probably doesn't belong.

### Words and pictures out of sync

When the narration talks about one thing while the slide shows another, the learner has to bridge the gap. Keep them aligned: talk about what's on screen, while it's on screen.

### Too much at once

A slide with a complex diagram, three bullet points and a table asks too much. Break it into steps. Reveal parts one at a time as the narration reaches them.

## Captions: helpful, with a caveat

Captions are essential for accessibility. They help learners who are deaf or hard of hearing, learners reading in a second language, and anyone watching without sound. They also help with unfamiliar terms.

There's a reasonable concern that on-screen text plus narration might overload some learners. In practice, the accessibility benefits are strong, and many learners rely on captions. A sensible approach is to keep the slide itself light on text, so captions are the main block of words on screen. There's more on this in [captions on presentation videos](/blog/captions-on-presentation-videos-why-and-how).

## Designing slides for explainer video learning

Here's how to put this into practice when you build or revise a teaching deck.

1. **Lead with a visual.** A diagram, chart, photo or simple shape arrangement. Something the narration can point at.
2. **Keep on-slide text short.** A headline that states the point and a few labels. Move the explanation into speaker notes.
3. **Write narration that refers to the visual.** "The arrow on the left shows..." "Notice the gap between these two bars."
4. **Reveal in steps.** If a diagram has four parts, build it up one part at a time.
5. **Cut what doesn't teach.** Remove decorative images and clip art.
6. **Keep each video short and focused.** One concept per video is easier to learn from and easier to find later.

## A before and after

**Before:** A slide titled "The water cycle" with a paragraph of text describing evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection, plus a small clip-art cloud. The narration reads the paragraph aloud.

**After:**

- **Headline:** "Water moves in a loop driven by the sun"
- **Visual:** A simple diagram with four labelled stages and arrows between them
- **Build:** Each stage appears as the narration reaches it
- **Speaker notes:**

> Start at the bottom, with the ocean. The sun heats the water, and some of it rises as vapour. That's evaporation. As the vapour rises, it cools and forms clouds. That's condensation. When the droplets get heavy enough, they fall as rain. And the water collects back in rivers and oceans, ready to start again.

The second version uses both channels well. The diagram carries the structure. The voice carries the explanation. The build keeps them in step.

## Key takeaways

- Words and pictures together are generally easier to learn from than words alone.
- Let the visual show and the narration explain. Don't read slide text aloud.
- Remove decorative material that doesn't teach.
- Keep narration and visuals in sync, and reveal complex visuals in steps.
- Use captions for accessibility, and keep slide text light so they don't crowd the screen.
