Ask three providers for a quote on an explainer video and you'll get three very different numbers. That's not because anyone is being unreasonable. Explainer video cost depends on what you're actually buying: strategy, scriptwriting, design, animation, voice talent, revisions and project management, or just some of those. This guide breaks down what drives the price, compares the main routes, and helps you pick one that fits your budget and your deadline.

We won't quote market prices here, because they vary widely by country, provider and scope, and change over time. Instead, use the factors below to read any quote properly.

## What drives explainer video cost

Every explainer video is made from the same parts. The price depends on who does each part and how much of it there is.

### Script and strategy

Someone has to work out what to say, to whom, and in what order. Agencies usually include discovery sessions and a scriptwriter. If you do it yourself, it's your time.

### Visual style

Custom character animation and bespoke illustration take the most time to produce. Motion graphics built from shapes and text take less. Animated slides take less again, because the design already exists.

### Length

Longer videos need more script, more visuals and more animation. Animation in particular tends to be priced by the second or minute of finished video.

### Voiceover

Professional voice talent adds a fee, and sometimes extra for usage rights. Recording your own voice costs time and a decent microphone. Text-to-speech voices remove that step.

### Revisions

A few rounds of changes are usually included. Extra rounds, or a change of direction late in the project, add to the bill.

### Music and footage

Licensed music and stock footage carry their own licence fees.

### Updates later on

This one is easy to forget. If your product, prices or policy change, how much does it cost to change the video? For agency-animated work, an update can mean going back to the studio.

## The three main routes compared

| Route | What you get | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Agency or studio | Strategy, script, custom design and animation, professional voice, project management | Highest spend, longer timelines, updates usually go back to the agency |
| Online video tools | Templates, stock media, built-in editing, often voice or avatar options | Subscription fees, learning curve, you still do the writing and assembly |
| Doing it yourself from slides | Your existing deck becomes the visuals; your speaker notes become the script | You own the writing and design; less custom than bespoke animation |

### When an agency is worth it

An agency makes sense when:

- The video is a flagship piece, such as the main video on your home page for the next couple of years.
- You need custom illustration or character animation that matches a strong brand.
- You don't have time or in-house skills for scripting and design.
- The message is stable and unlikely to change soon.

### When online tools fit

General video tools suit teams who make a lot of varied video content, want stock footage and music, and are comfortable editing on a timeline.

### When doing it yourself from slides fits

Making explainers from your own decks works well when:

- You already have a clear, on-brand deck.
- You need several videos rather than one perfect one.
- The content changes often, such as product features, onboarding or pricing.
- You want to test a message before investing in a bigger production.

## Hidden costs to ask about

Whichever route you choose, ask:

- How many revision rounds are included, and what does an extra round cost?
- Who owns the final files and the source files?
- Are music, footage and voice licensed for all the places you'll use the video?
- What does it cost to update the video in six months?
- What formats and sizes are delivered (landscape, square, vertical)?

## A worked example: one message, three ways

Say a small software company wants a 90-second video explaining its new invoicing feature.

- **Agency:** A discovery call, a script, storyboard, custom animation and a professional voiceover over several weeks. The result is polished and distinctive. When the feature changes next quarter, an update is a new job.
- **Online tool:** The marketer spends a few days choosing a template, sourcing stock clips and music, and assembling the edit. Flexible, but time-hungry, and there's a subscription.
- **Slides:** The marketer takes the six feature slides from the sales deck, rewrites the speaker notes as a script, and exports a narrated video with a motion style and captions. It's ready the same day. When the feature changes, she edits the slides and exports again.

None of these is wrong. They suit different goals. Many teams use a mix: one agency flagship, plus a steady stream of slide-based explainers for everything else. ## Make your budget go further

- **Write the script first.** A clear script cuts revision rounds on any route.
- **Start from what you have.** Your sales or webinar deck is already designed and approved.
- **Keep it short.** Shorter videos cost less to make and are easier to update.
- **Plan for change.** Choose a route that makes updates cheap if your content moves quickly.

## Key takeaways

- Explainer video cost depends on scripting, style, length, voice, revisions, licensing and future updates.
- Agencies suit stable flagship videos; slide-based videos suit frequent, changing content.
- Always ask about revisions, ownership, licensing and update costs.
