The voice is the first thing viewers judge in a narrated video. A good text to speech narrator voice disappears into the background and lets the content do the talking. A poor match, or a speed that's slightly off, makes even good slides harder to follow. This post covers how to choose a voice, set the speed and write a script that sounds natural.

## Where text-to-speech voices come from

Most browsers and operating systems include built-in text-to-speech voices. The selection depends on your device, operating system and browser. A voice you see on a Windows laptop in Edge may not appear on a Mac in Safari or on a Chromebook.

PowerPoint2Vid uses these built-in browser voices to read your speaker notes. That means:

- **Your voice list depends on your device.** Two colleagues may see different options.
- **Export on the machine with the voice you like.** If you switch computers, the narration may sound different.
- **Some browsers offer more natural-sounding voices than others.** It's worth comparing the same script in two browsers if you have them.

## How to choose a text to speech narrator voice

### Match the voice to the audience

Think about who's listening.

- **Students** often do well with a clear, steady voice that doesn't rush technical terms.
- **Customers** respond to a warm, conversational tone.
- **Staff in training** need clarity above all, especially for policy wording.

### Match the accent where you can

If your audience is mostly in one region, a voice with a familiar accent can feel more natural. Many devices include English voices for several regions, such as Australia, the UK and the US. Check pronunciation of local place names and terms.

### Test with your own script

Don't judge a voice on its sample sentence. Paste in a real paragraph from your notes, including the hard bits: names, numbers, acronyms and technical terms.

Here's a short test script you can adapt:

"Welcome to the induction for new staff at the Joondalup campus. This module takes about six minutes. We'll cover three things: where to find your timetable, how to book a room, and who to call if something goes wrong. Let's start with your timetable."

Listen for place names, numbers and whether the pauses fall in sensible places.

### Compare two or three, not twenty

Shortlist a few voices and play the same paragraph through each. Ask a colleague to listen too. Pick the one that's easiest to follow, not the one that sounds most impressive.

## Setting the speaking speed

Speed matters as much as the voice. Too slow feels patronising. Too fast loses people.

| Content | Suggested setting |
|---|---|
| Dense technical or academic material | A little slower than default |
| Policy, compliance or instructions | Default or slightly slower |
| Product overviews and marketing | Default |
| Short social clips with captions | Default or slightly faster |

These are starting points, not rules. Preview a full slide and adjust.

### Signs the speed is wrong

- **Too fast:** captions flick past before you can read them; you find yourself rewinding.
- **Too slow:** you're ahead of the narrator; you want to skip.

### Speed and slide timing

In PowerPoint2Vid, timing follows the speech, so each slide stays up as long as its narration lasts. Slow the voice and every slide gets longer. If a slide has very little narration, set the "minimum seconds per slide" so it doesn't flash past.

## Write for the voice, not the page

Text-to-speech reads exactly what you write. A few habits make any voice sound better.

- **Short sentences.** They give the voice natural places to pause.
- **Spoken numbers.** "About two thousand" sounds better than "approx. 2,000".
- **Spell out abbreviations** the first time: "the Learning Management System, or LMS".
- **Avoid symbols.** Write "and" instead of "&", and "per cent" instead of "%" if the voice reads symbols awkwardly.
- **Use full stops for pauses.** A comma gives a short pause; a full stop gives a longer one.

**Before:** "Q3 rev. up 8% vs Q2 & ahead of target (incl. new accts)."

**After:** "Revenue in the third quarter rose eight per cent on the second quarter. That puts us ahead of target, including new accounts."

If a word still sounds wrong, spell it the way it's said. For example, a surname might work better written phonetically in the notes. Remember that the captions show what's in your script, so check both.

## When to use no narration

Not every video needs a voice. PowerPoint2Vid has a "no narration" option for silent videos, useful for looping displays or social clips where most people won't turn the sound on. Pair it with clear headlines and a sensible minimum time per slide.

## Quick checklist

- Shortlist two or three voices available on your device
- Test each with a real paragraph from your notes
- Check names, numbers and acronyms
- Set the speed for the content and audience
- Rewrite notes in short, spoken sentences
- Set a minimum time per slide for quiet slides
- Export on the device with the voice you chose
