Your thumbnail is the first thing people see, and often the only thing they judge before deciding whether to watch. A great video with a muddy thumbnail gets scrolled past. These video thumbnail tips are written for marketers and founders sharing explainer and presentation videos, but they apply to any video you post on YouTube, LinkedIn, your website or an email.

The short version: one idea, a few bold words, strong contrast, and a design that still works at the size of a postage stamp.

## Why the thumbnail matters so much

On most platforms, people see a grid or feed of small images with titles. They decide in a moment. The thumbnail has to:

- Show what the video is about.
- Stand out from everything around it.
- Make a promise the video keeps.

That last point matters. A thumbnail that overpromises might get a click, but viewers leave quickly when the video doesn't match. Aim for honest and clear, not shocking.

## Video thumbnail tips: the basics

### Design for small screens first

Most thumbnails are seen small, often on a phone. Before you finalise one, shrink it right down on your screen. If you can't read the words or tell what the image is, simplify.

### One idea per thumbnail

The same rule that works for slides works here. One focal point, one message. A thumbnail with a chart, a logo, a face, three icons and a sentence says nothing at a glance.

### Three to five words, maximum

Text on a thumbnail should be short and big. It doesn't need to repeat the title. It should add to it.

- Title: "How to Cut Onboarding Time for New Customers"
- Thumbnail text: "Setup in 10 minutes"

The title explains; the thumbnail hooks.

### Strong contrast

Bold text on a plain or blurred background reads best. Dark text on light, or light on dark. Add a solid shape behind the words if the background is busy. Aim for the same contrast standards you'd use on slides: WCAG AA (4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text) is a sensible floor, and thumbnails usually need more.

### Use a consistent look

If you post a series, give every thumbnail the same layout, font and colour scheme. People start to recognise your videos in a feed before they read anything.

## What to put on a thumbnail for a slide video

Presentation and explainer videos have an advantage: you already have strong visuals in your deck. Some reliable options:

### The key number

If your video is built around a result, put the number front and centre.

- Example: a large "3x" with the words "faster quotes" underneath.

### The before and after

Split the thumbnail in two: a messy slide on the left, a clean one on the right, with "Before" and "After" labels. Great for how-to and tips videos.

### The one diagram

If your video explains a process, a simplified version of the key diagram works well. Strip it to three or four boxes and make the labels huge.

### A face, if it's your brand

If you or a team member is the recognisable face of the brand, a clear photo with a natural expression can help. Keep it large and well lit, with the face filling a good part of the frame.

## How to make a thumbnail from your deck

You don't need special design software.

1. **Duplicate a strong slide** from your deck, or start a new 16:9 slide so it matches the usual video thumbnail shape.
2. **Strip it back.** Remove body text, footers and slide numbers. Keep one visual and one short line.
3. **Make the text much bigger** than you would on a normal slide. Think headline size, then go bigger.
4. **Boost contrast.** Darken or blur a busy background, or use a solid colour.
5. **Export the slide as an image.** PowerPoint, Google Slides and Keynote can all save a slide as a PNG or JPEG. Look for the Export, Save As or Download option.
6. **Check it small.** Shrink the image to thumbnail size on your screen and see if it still reads.

Platforms have their own requirements for custom thumbnails, such as size limits and whether your account can upload one. Check the platform's current help pages before you upload.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Using the default frame.** Platforms often pick a random frame from your video. It's rarely the best one. Upload a custom thumbnail where you can.
- **Too much text.** If it takes more than a second to read, it's too much.
- **Tiny logos and fine print.** They vanish at thumbnail size.
- **Misleading images.** A thumbnail that doesn't match the video damages trust.
- **Ignoring the title.** Thumbnail and title work as a pair. Write them together.

## Test and improve

Treat your first thumbnail as a draft. Once a video has been up for a while, look at how often it gets clicked compared with how often it's shown, if your platform reports it. If a video is getting shown but not clicked, the thumbnail and title are the first things to change. ## Quick checklist

- One idea, one focal point.
- Three to five words of big, bold text.
- Strong contrast; solid shape behind text on busy backgrounds.
- Readable when shrunk to phone size.
- Consistent layout across a series.
- Honest: the video delivers what the thumbnail promises.
- Written together with the title.
