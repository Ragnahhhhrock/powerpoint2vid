Fonts do quiet work on a slide. When they're right, nobody notices them. When they're wrong, people squint, lose the thread, or stop reading. The best fonts for presentations aren't the most interesting ones. They're the ones that stay readable at a distance, on a small screen, and in a compressed video.

This guide covers the three decisions that matter most: which typefaces to use, how to pair them, and how big to set them.

## What makes a font readable on screen

Slides are viewed in tough conditions. A projector washes out contrast. A laptop shrinks everything. A video file adds compression. A good presentation font holds up in all three.

Look for these qualities:

- **Open letter shapes.** Letters like "a", "e" and "c" have clear gaps, so they don't blur together.
- **Generous x-height.** Lowercase letters are tall relative to capitals, which makes text look bigger at the same point size.
- **Distinct characters.** Capital "I", lowercase "l" and the number "1" look different from each other.
- **Solid strokes.** Very thin strokes disappear when shrunk or compressed.

Most clean sans-serif typefaces meet these tests. Many serif faces do too, especially those designed for screens.

## The best fonts for presentations: safe, clear choices

You don't need a special font. You need a reliable one that renders the same on every device.

### Fonts that come with most systems

Fonts bundled with common operating systems and office suites are the safest choice. They're installed almost everywhere, so your deck looks the same when someone else opens it. Calibri, Arial, Segoe UI, Helvetica and Verdana are common examples. Georgia is a well-known serif built for screens.

### Fonts from Google Fonts

If you work in Google Slides, families like Roboto, Open Sans, Lato, Source Sans and Inter are easy to use and widely available. If you download the deck as .pptx, check the fonts carried over correctly, because a font not installed on the next device may be replaced.

### Fonts to avoid for body text

- Script and handwriting fonts (fine for one word, hard to read for a sentence)
- Condensed or narrow faces at small sizes
- Very light or thin weights
- Novelty display fonts

## Pairing fonts without clashing

Most decks need only one or two typefaces. More than that starts to look busy.

### Option 1: One family, several weights

The simplest approach. Use the bold weight for headlines and the regular weight for body text. It always looks consistent, and it's hard to get wrong.

### Option 2: A headline font plus a body font

If you want more personality, pair a distinctive headline font with a plain body font. A few rules keep this safe:

- **Contrast, don't compete.** Pair a serif with a sans-serif, or a heavy display face with a light, neutral one. Two similar sans-serifs side by side look like a mistake.
- **Let the body font be boring.** It carries most of the reading. Plain is good.
- **Limit the headline font to headlines.** Don't use it for captions, labels or body text.

### Sample pairings

- Georgia headlines with Arial body
- Montserrat Bold headlines with Open Sans body
- Inter Bold headlines with Inter Regular body (one family)

## How big should slide text be?

Size is where most decks go wrong. Text that looks fine on your monitor at arm's length is often too small for a projector or a phone screen.

| Element | Suggested size |
|---|---|
| Slide headline | 36 to 44pt |
| Body text | 24pt or larger |
| Chart labels and captions | 18pt minimum |
| Source notes and footnotes | 14 to 16pt, used sparingly |

If text won't fit at 24pt, the slide has too much on it. Cut words or split the slide. Move the detail into speaker notes, where it can become narration instead.

### Bigger still for video

When a deck becomes a video, people often watch it on a phone or in a small embedded player. Text that's readable at full screen can become hard to read in a small window. If your slides are headed for video, lean towards the larger end of each range.

## Spacing and alignment

Readability isn't just about the font. How you set it matters too.

- **Line spacing.** Around 1.1 to 1.3 times the font size gives lines room to breathe without falling apart.
- **Line length.** Keep lines short. A full-width line of small text across a 16:9 slide is tiring to read.
- **Left alignment.** Left-aligned text is easier to read than centred text for anything longer than a line or two.
- **Case.** Sentence case reads more easily than all caps. Save capitals for short labels.

## A before and after

**Before:** A slide with the headline "Q2 Results" in a decorative script font at 28pt, and seven bullet points in a light-weight font at 16pt, centred.

**After:**

- Headline in a bold sans-serif at 40pt: "Q2 sales grew in every region except the west"
- One chart, labelled directly at 18pt
- One supporting line of body text at 24pt, left-aligned
- The other six bullet points moved into the speaker notes

The second slide is easier to read in a room, on a laptop and in a video. It also says more, because the headline now makes a point. There's more on that in [slide headlines that make a point](/blog/slide-headlines-that-make-a-point).

## Key takeaways

- Choose a plain, well-made font that renders the same everywhere.
- Use one family with two weights, or a contrasting pair at most.
- Set body text at 24pt or larger, headlines at 36pt or larger.
- Keep line spacing comfortable and align body text left.
- If it doesn't fit at a readable size, cut or split the slide.
- Check fonts after downloading or exporting to .pptx.

## Fonts that hold up in your slide video

When a deck becomes a video, every font decision is tested on smaller screens and through compression. Large, plain, high-contrast type survives that well. Thin, small, decorative type doesn't.
