Your onboarding deck works well in English. Now the business has a team in Jakarta, a warehouse crew whose first language is Vietnamese, and contractors who'd follow along much better in Spanish. Recording a separate voiceover for each language is slow and costly. If your videos are built from slides and speaker notes, though, you can translate presentation video content by translating the deck, then exporting it again.

This guide covers how to set up a deck for translation, what to check and how to keep several language versions in step.

## Why it's easier to translate presentation video built from slides

With a filmed video, translating means re-recording or adding subtitles over someone speaking English. With a slide video, the words live in two places you control:

- **Slide text:** headlines, labels, captions on charts
- **Speaker notes:** the script the narrator reads aloud

Translate both, choose a voice in the target language, and you have a new version. Change the policy next quarter and you update each deck and export again. No studio bookings, no voice talent schedules.

## Step 1: Get the source deck right first

Translation multiplies every problem in the original. Fix these before anything goes to a translator.

- **Lock the content.** Make sure the English version is approved. Changes after translation mean paying for, or doing, the work again.
- **Use plain language.** Short sentences, common words, no idioms. "Hit the ground running" doesn't translate well. "Start work quickly" does.
- **Leave room on slides.** Many languages take more space than English. A headline that just fits in English may overflow in German or Spanish. Keep generous margins.
- **Avoid text inside images.** Words baked into a picture can't be edited. Use real text boxes over images instead.
- **Put names and numbers in consistent formats.** Decide how dates, currency and units should appear for each audience.

## Step 2: Translate slide text and speaker notes

You have a few options, and the right one depends on the content.

| Content type | Sensible approach |
|---|---|
| Compliance, safety, legal | Professional translator, then review by a native-speaking subject expert |
| Onboarding and process training | Machine translation as a draft, reviewed by a fluent colleague |
| Internal updates | Machine translation with a quick fluent check |

Whichever route you take, translate the speaker notes as carefully as the slide text. The notes are what people hear, so they carry most of the meaning.

Give your translator the whole deck, not a spreadsheet of disconnected sentences. Context matters. "Clear the area" means something different on a safety slide than on a desk-tidying slide.

## Step 3: Save each language as its own .pptx

Keep one file per language, named clearly:

- `onboarding-v4-en.pptx`
- `onboarding-v4-es.pptx`
- `onboarding-v4-vi.pptx`

Keep the version number in every file name. When the English source changes to v5, you can see straight away which translations need updating.

## Step 4: Check the voice for each language

Narration is read by a synthetic voice, and the quality of voices varies from language to language. Before you plan a language version, check the voices on offer for your language and confirm there's a suitable one.

Then preview a few slides and listen closely:

- Does the voice match the language of the notes? An English voice reading Spanish text sounds wrong.
- Are names, product terms and acronyms pronounced sensibly? If not, rephrase or spell them the way they should sound.
- Is the speed right? Adjust the speaking speed for each language version if needed.

If you can't find a voice you're happy with for a language, consider whether captions with a clear, simple script, or a human voiceover for that version, would serve your audience better.

## Step 5: Turn on captions

Captions are especially useful for multilingual audiences. Viewers can read along while they listen, which helps with technical terms and accents.

Captions follow the narration script, so they'll be in the same language as your translated notes.

## Step 6: Review with a native speaker

This step is not optional. Ask someone fluent in the language, ideally from the audience you're training, to watch the full video and check:

- Is the translation accurate and natural?
- Does anything sound awkward when spoken aloud?
- Do any slides have text overflowing or cut off?
- Are cultural examples appropriate?

Fix what they find in the deck, then export again.

## A worked example

**English speaker note:**

> Before you start your shift, sign in at the kiosk by the front door. If the kiosk is down, tell your supervisor straight away.

**Simplified for translation:**

> Before your shift starts, sign in at the kiosk next to the front door. If the kiosk is not working, tell your supervisor immediately.

The second version swaps "is down" and "straight away" for plainer phrases that translate more reliably.

## Quick checklist

- Source deck approved and written in plain language
- Space left for longer text, no words inside images
- Slide text and speaker notes both translated
- One clearly versioned .pptx per language
- Suitable voice confirmed for each language
- Captions on
- Native-speaker review completed
