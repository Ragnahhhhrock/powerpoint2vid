Keynote makes beautiful slides, and sooner or later many Keynote users need those slides as a video. Maybe it is a pre-recorded conference talk, a lecture, or a project update for people in other time zones. Going from Keynote to video is straightforward once you know your two main options and which one suits your situation.

This guide covers both, step by step, and helps you pick.

## Two routes from Keynote to video

1. **Keynote's own movie export.** Keynote can export a presentation as a movie file. This works well if you have recorded your own narration and timings in Keynote, or if a silent, self-running video is enough.
2. **Export to PowerPoint, then convert.** Keynote can export a presentation as a PowerPoint file. You can then turn that .pptx into a narrated video, using your presenter notes as the script and a text-to-speech voice to read them.

Neither is better in every case. Here is how they compare.

| | Keynote movie export | Export to .pptx, then convert |
|---|---|---|
| Narration | Your own recorded voice, or none | Text-to-speech reads your presenter notes |
| Timing | Recorded timings or fixed intervals | Follows the narration, with a minimum per slide |
| Look | Keynote's own transitions and builds | One of six video styles, plus transitions |
| Updating | Re-record narration if it changes | Edit the note, export again |
| Captions | Add separately | Can be burned into the video |
| Where it runs | Keynote on Mac, iPad or iPhone | A web browser |

## Option 1: Use Keynote's own export

If you are happy to record your voice, or the video can be silent, Keynote's built-in export is the simplest route.

### Steps
1. Open the presentation in Keynote.
2. If you want narration, use Keynote's recording feature to record your voice as you advance the slides.
3. Look for the Export option in the File menu and choose a movie format.
4. Choose whether to use your recorded timings or a fixed interval between slides and builds.
5. Pick a resolution and export.

### Good for
- Talks where your own voice matters, such as a personal pitch or an assessed presentation.
- Decks that rely heavily on Keynote-specific animations.

### Watch out for
- Recording a clean narration can take several takes.
- Any later change to the script means recording that part again.

## Option 2: Export to PowerPoint and convert

If you would rather not record yourself, or the content will change and need re-exporting, the second route is usually faster.

### Step 1: Prepare your presenter notes
Your presenter notes become the narration. Before exporting, read through them:

- Write in short, spoken sentences.
- Spell out acronyms or write them the way they sound.
- Remove URLs, symbols and stage directions like "pause here".

Sample presenter note, before and after:

Before: "Results -> see fig. 2. Approx 3x improvement vs baseline (p<0.05). Next: methods."

After: "Here are the results. The new approach performed about three times better than the baseline. The difference was statistically significant. Next, I'll explain how we tested it."

### Step 2: Export from Keynote as PowerPoint
Look for the Export option and choose PowerPoint. Keynote saves a .pptx copy of your presentation, including presenter notes.

### Step 3: Check the exported file
Open the .pptx if you can, or go straight to the preview. Watch for:

- **Fonts.** Some Mac fonts may be swapped for others when the deck is opened elsewhere. Common fonts travel best.
- **Animations.** Complex Keynote builds may not carry over exactly. Simple slides convert most reliably.
- **Notes.** Confirm the presenter notes came across with each slide.

### Step 4: Turn the .pptx into a video
1. Drop the .pptx into PowerPoint2Vid. It runs in your browser, so your slides stay on your device.
2. Choose the narration script. The default reads speaker notes, falling back to slide text on slides without notes.
3. Pick a voice and set the speaking speed. On a Mac, the voices available depend on your browser and system.
4. Set a minimum seconds per slide.
5. Choose a style. Keynote launch places each slide as a rounded card on a dark stage, which suits many Keynote decks. Minimal cinematic adds a slow push-in and lower-third captions.
6. Choose a transition: crossfade, slide, zoom or none.
7. Turn on burned-in captions if you want them.
8. Preview with the voice on, then export with the tab in the foreground.

For Chrome, if you want narration in the file, choose "this tab" and tick "share tab audio" when prompted. The output is usually WebM, or MP4 in browsers that support it. There is more Mac-specific detail in [how to convert a PowerPoint to video on a Mac](/blog/how-to-convert-a-powerpoint-to-video-on-a-mac).

## Which route should you choose?

Choose **Keynote's export** if:
- Your own voice is required or important.
- Your deck depends on Keynote-specific animation.

Choose **export to .pptx and convert** if:
- You would rather not record yourself.
- The script may change and you want quick re-exports.
- You want captions burned in and a styled video look.

Many presenters use both: Keynote's export for a personal keynote, and a narrated slide video for supporting material.

## Quick checklist

- Presenter notes rewritten for the ear
- Deck exported as .pptx
- Fonts and notes checked after export
- Narration script, voice and speed chosen
- Minimum seconds per slide set
- Style and transition picked
- Captions on if needed
- Full preview watched before export
