You spend weeks on a client report. Forty pages, an appendix, a findings deck. Then it lands in an executive's inbox, and they skim the summary page between meetings. A consulting report video changes that. A short, narrated walkthrough of your key findings gives busy stakeholders the story in a few minutes, in your words, even when you're not in the room.

This playbook covers when a video summary helps, how to build one from the deck you already have, and how to send it so it gets watched.

## Why a video summary helps

Reports are thorough by design. That's their value, and also why they get skimmed. A short video does a different job:

- **It tells the story in order.** Situation, finding, implication, recommendation. No skipping to page 31.
- **It reaches people who weren't in the readout.** The CFO who missed the meeting. The board member who'll read it the night before.
- **It keeps your framing.** When a report gets forwarded, your nuance often gets lost. A narrated summary carries your emphasis with it.
- **It fits async work.** Clients in different time zones or with packed calendars can watch when it suits them. See [async video vs live meetings](/blog/async-video-vs-live-meetings-when-a-video-saves-everyone-time) for more on that trade-off.

A video summary doesn't replace the report or the meeting. It's the bridge between them.

## When to use a consulting report video

Good moments:

- **Before a readout meeting**, so attendees arrive already knowing the headlines and the meeting goes straight to discussion.
- **After a readout**, for people who couldn't attend or need a refresher.
- **At handover**, when your engagement ends and the client team takes over implementation.
- **For interim updates**, when a short "here's where we are" saves a status call.

Less suitable: highly sensitive findings that need to be delivered in person, or early drafts the client hasn't agreed to circulate.

## Build the video from your findings deck

You almost certainly have a findings deck already. Don't convert all of it. Build a short summary version.

### Step 1: Pick the five to eight slides that matter

Aim for a video of a few minutes. A useful structure:

1. **What we were asked.** One slide restating the brief.
2. **What we found.** Two to four slides, one finding each.
3. **What it means.** One slide on implications.
4. **What we recommend.** One slide with the top recommendations.
5. **What happens next.** One slide with the decision or action needed.

### Step 2: Rewrite headlines as findings

Consulting decks often already do this well. If yours don't, fix them now.

- Before: "Procurement analysis"
- After: "Three suppliers account for most late deliveries"

Each headline should make sense on its own. An executive who only watches with the sound off should still get the gist.

### Step 3: Simplify the visuals

Report charts are often built for reading closely. For video, strip them back:

- One message per chart.
- Highlight the bar, line or segment that matters.
- Label directly; drop the legend.
- Move supporting tables to the appendix.

### Step 4: Write the speaker notes as your voiceover

The notes are what viewers will hear. Write them the way you'd explain the finding across a table.

Here's a sample for a findings slide:

Slide headline: "Three suppliers account for most late deliveries"

Speaker notes:

> "When we traced every late delivery from the last twelve months, a pattern stood out. Most of them came from just three suppliers. That's good news, because it means the fix is focused. You don't need to overhaul procurement. You need a different conversation with three vendors."

Short sentences. The point first. The implication last. No jargon the client wouldn't use themselves.

### Step 5: Add a clear ask at the end

Finish with exactly what you need from the viewer. "We'd like a decision on option B at the steering committee on the 14th." Leave it on screen.

## Choosing the look and voice

A client video should feel calm and professional.

- **Look:** Keep the source slides restrained and professional. The video builds on them, so calm slides give a calm result.
- **Transitions:** Crossfade or none. Nothing that distracts from the findings.
- **Voice:** Try a few voices and pick one that sounds measured. Adjust the speaking speed so it doesn't feel rushed.
- **Captions:** Turn them on. Executives often watch with the sound off.
- **Branding:** Use your firm's slide master so the video matches the report.

## Confidentiality matters

Client reports often contain sensitive information. Before you make any video, check your engagement terms and the client's preferences about how findings are shared.

Practical points:

- Check your firm's policy and the client's terms on using online tools before you upload any part of the report, and leave out anything you are not permitted to share.
- Share the finished video through the same secure channel you use for the report, such as the client's document portal or your firm's file-sharing system.
- Mark the title slide with the client name, date and a confidentiality note.

## Sending it so it gets watched

How you send it matters as much as what's in it.

- **Put the length in the message.** "A 4-minute summary of our findings" gets opened.
- **Say why they should watch.** "So we can use Thursday's meeting for decisions, not slides."
- **Send it to the right people.** Ask your sponsor who needs to see it before the readout.
- **Attach or link the full report** for anyone who wants the detail.

A sample email line:

> "Ahead of Thursday's steering committee, here's a 4-minute video summary of our findings and the two decisions we need. The full report is attached for reference."

## Quick checklist

- Pick five to eight slides: brief, findings, implications, recommendations, next step.
- Rewrite every headline as a finding.
- Strip charts back to one message each.
- Write the notes the way you'd explain it in person.
- End with one clear ask.
- Choose a calm style, crossfade transition and captions.
- Check confidentiality terms and share through a secure channel.
- Tell people how long it is and why it's worth watching.
