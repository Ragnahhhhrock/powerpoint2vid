Every account team has a list of questions it answers again and again. How do I add a user? What happens at renewal? Where do I find the invoice? If you want to reduce support tickets with video, start with that list. A short narrated explainer answers the same question the same way every time, and it keeps working while you sleep.

This post shows how to find the questions worth answering, how to build a short video for each one from slides, and where to put those videos so people find them before they write in.

## Why repeat questions keep coming back

Repeat questions rarely mean your customers are careless. Usually they mean one of three things:

- **The answer is hard to find.** It lives in a long help article, an old email thread or someone's head.
- **The answer is hard to follow in text.** Steps with screens, settings and order of operations are easier to see than to read.
- **The answer changed.** A process was updated, but the explanation people remember was not.

Text documentation still matters. But for anything with a sequence or a "where do I click" element, a narrated walkthrough is often quicker to follow. Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. That is the core of why a short explainer can stop a question from coming back.

## How to reduce support tickets with video: pick the right questions

Not every question deserves a video. Spend your effort where it pays back.

### Step 1: Pull the list

Look through your inbox, your support queue or your CRM notes from the last quarter. Write down every question you or your team answered more than a few times. Group near-duplicates together.

### Step 2: Score each question

For each group, ask:

1. How often does it come up?
2. How long does a good answer take to write?
3. Does the answer involve steps, screens or a diagram?
4. Is the answer stable, or does it change every month?

The best candidates come up often, take a while to answer, involve steps, and stay stable. A question like "How do I export my monthly report?" usually ticks every box.

### Step 3: Start with five

Pick the top five. Five short videos you actually finish will do more than twenty you plan and never ship.

## Build each answer as a short slide video

Each video should answer one question. Keep it to a handful of slides and a couple of minutes at most.

### A simple structure

- **Slide 1: The question, in the customer's words.** "How do I export my monthly report?"
- **Slides 2 to 5: The steps.** One step per slide, with a screenshot and the one thing to notice highlighted.
- **Last slide: What to do if it doesn't work.** One line, plus where to go next.

### Write the notes the way you'd say them

The speaker notes become the narration, so write them as you would talk a customer through it on a call.

Here is a sample speaker note for a step slide:

> "Open the Reports tab at the top of the screen. You'll see a list of your saved reports. Find the one called Monthly Summary, and click the three dots on the right."

Compare that with a typical help-article line: "Navigate to Reports and select the overflow menu for the relevant report." The first version is what a person would say. It is also easier to follow when heard.

### Use the slide for the picture, not the paragraph

Put the screenshot and a short label on the slide. Keep the full sentence in the notes. If the viewer can pause and see exactly which button to press, you have done the job.

## Before and after: one real-world style rewrite

**Before** (email reply, sent fifteen times last month):

> "Hi, to update billing details go to Settings, then Account, then Billing, and update the card on file. If you're on an annual plan the change will apply at renewal. Let me know if you have issues."

**After** (three-slide video):

- Slide 1 headline: "Updating your billing details takes under a minute"
- Slide 2: Screenshot of the Settings menu, with Billing circled. Note: "Open Settings, then choose Billing from the menu on the left."
- Slide 3: Screenshot of the card form. Note: "Enter your new card and press Save. If you pay yearly, the change applies at your next renewal."

Now the reply becomes one line and a link. The customer sees exactly where to go, and your team gets that time back.

## Put the videos where questions start

A video nobody finds does not cut any tickets. Place each one at the point where the question usually comes up:

- **In your help centre**, at the top of the matching article.
- **In onboarding emails**, linked from the step it explains.
- **In your email signature or saved replies**, so the team can send it in one click.
- **Inside the product**, if you have a help panel or tooltip that can link out.
- **In handover notes** when a new customer moves from sales to customer success.

## Keep the videos accurate

The fastest way to create new questions is an out-of-date video. Build upkeep into the process:

- Keep the source deck in a shared folder with a clear name.
- Note the date and product version on the last slide.
- When the product changes, update the screenshot and the note, then export again.

Because the narration comes from your notes, a change does not need anyone to re-record their voice. Edit the words, export the video, replace the link.

## How to tell if it's working

You don't need a dashboard to see a change. Keep it simple:

- Count how often each question came in during the month before the video, then the month after.
- Ask your team whether they are sending the link instead of writing the answer.
- Watch for follow-up questions. If people still ask the same thing after watching, the video needs a clearer step.

## Quick checklist

- List the questions your team answers most often.
- Pick five that are frequent, step-based and stable.
- One question per video, a handful of slides, short notes written for the ear.
- One step per slide, with the key button or field highlighted.
- Place each video where the question usually starts.
- Update the deck and re-export when the process changes.
