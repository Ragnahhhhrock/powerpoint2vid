LinkedIn is where your buyers, clients and peers already spend time at work. A short, narrated presentation video on LinkedIn can explain your idea while you're busy elsewhere, and it reaches people a PDF attachment never would. This guide covers how to prepare the video, how to write the post around it, and how to turn views into conversations.

## Why share a presentation video on LinkedIn

A slide deck on its own asks a lot of the reader. They have to click, open, and work out the story by themselves. A narrated video tells the story for them.

For sellers, consultants and customer success teams, that means:

- **Your explanation travels without you.** Prospects can watch, then forward it to a colleague.
- **Your expertise is visible.** A clear two-minute explainer shows you understand the problem.
- **It starts conversations.** A good video gives people something specific to comment on or message you about.

## Prepare the video before you post

### Keep it short

Feeds move quickly. Pick one idea and cut everything else. If your deck has 25 slides, choose the four to eight that make a single point. Save them as a separate short deck.

### Open strong

The first slide decides whether people keep watching. Skip the title slide with your logo. Open with the problem or the payoff instead.

**Before:** "Acme Logistics: Capability Overview"
**After:** "Why late deliveries usually start in the warehouse, not on the road"

Our post on [how to open a presentation so people keep watching](/blog/how-to-open-a-presentation-so-people-keep-watching) has more ideas for first slides.

### Design for sound off

Many people scroll LinkedIn with the sound off, especially at work. So:

- Burn captions into the video.
- Make every slide headline readable on a phone.
- Keep text large and high contrast.

### Check the file

LinkedIn accepts common video formats such as MP4. If your video exported as WebM, convert it to MP4 with a common tool before uploading, or check whether LinkedIn accepts it directly at the time you post. Platforms change their supported formats, so check their current help page if an upload fails.

## Write the post around the video

The video is only half the post. The text above it does a lot of the work.

### The first line

Only the first line or two show before "see more". Make it count.

- State the problem: "Most onboarding programs lose new customers in week two."
- Or the payoff: "A two-minute walkthrough of how we cut our month-end close to three days."

### The body

Keep it short. Two to four sentences that give context and say what the video covers. Use line breaks so it's easy to scan.

### The ask

End with one clear action. Invite a specific reply rather than a vague "thoughts?".

### Sample post

> Most onboarding programs lose new customers in week two, not week one.
>
> I put together a two-minute walkthrough of the three moments where we see people drop off, and what we changed at each one.
>
> If you run onboarding for a SaaS product, I'd like to hear which of the three you see most.

## Posting options compared

There are a few ways to put a presentation on LinkedIn. Each has its place.

| Option | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Native video upload | Reach in the feed, sound-off viewing with burned-in captions | Keep it short; landscape slides appear smaller on phones |
| Link to a video on YouTube or your site | Longer videos, sending traffic to your site | Link previews often get less attention than native video |
| Document or PDF post | Detailed reference slides people swipe through | No narration; the story depends on the slides alone |
| Direct message with a link | One-to-one follow-up with a prospect | Personalise the message; don't mass-send |

Many people do both: post the native video publicly, then send a personal message with the link to the few people it's most relevant to.

## Turn views into conversations

A post that gets views but no conversations hasn't done its job yet.

- **Reply to every comment** in the first few hours. Ask a follow-up question.
- **Message the right people.** If a prospect's colleague likes the post, that's a reason to reach out with a short, relevant note.
- **Reuse it.** Pin it to your profile's featured section if it represents your work well. Send it in follow-up emails after calls.
- **Make the next one.** If a video gets good questions, those questions are your next video.

## Common mistakes

- Posting the whole deck as one long video.
- A first line that just says "Check out our new video".
- Small text that only works on a laptop.
- No captions.
- Posting and disappearing instead of replying to comments.

## Quick checklist

- One idea, four to eight slides.
- A first slide that names the problem or payoff.
- Captions burned in, large readable text.
- A file format LinkedIn accepts.
- A first line that works on its own.
- One clear ask at the end of the post.
- Time set aside to reply to comments.

## Make the video from your deck first

Before any of this, you need the video. Drop your short deck into the [PowerPoint to video converter](/) and your speaker notes become narration in the browser's built-in voice. Turn on burned-in captions for sound-off viewing, pick a style that suits your brand, and preview the result before exporting. The slides never leave your device, which helps when the deck includes client details you'd rather keep private.
