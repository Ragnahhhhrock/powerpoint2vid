Knowing how to start a presentation is half the battle. The first thirty seconds decide whether people lean in or reach for their phones. In a video, the stakes are higher still: one click and they're gone. Yet most presentations open with an agenda, a bio and a thank-you. This guide shows you better openings and gives you sample lines you can adapt.

## Why openings matter more on video

In a room, an audience gives you a few minutes of polite attention. On video, viewers decide quickly whether to keep watching. They can skip ahead, switch tabs or close the window without anyone noticing.

That means the opening should answer one question fast: "Why should I watch this?"

## What to cut from the start

Before adding anything, remove what slows the opening down.

- **The agenda slide as slide two.** Viewers don't need a table of contents before they know why they care.
- **Your full bio.** A name and one line of relevance is enough. Save the rest for the end, or skip it.
- **Thanks and housekeeping.** Useful live, not in a recording.
- **Apologies.** "I'll try to keep this short" or "Bear with me" set a weak tone.
- **Long definitions.** Explain terms when you need them, not up front.

## How to start a presentation: five patterns that work

### 1. Start with the problem

Name a problem your audience already feels.

> "Every semester, the same twenty questions land in your inbox in week one."

This works well for training, teaching and product talks. The viewer recognises the situation and wants the answer.

### 2. Start with the result

Give away the conclusion first, then explain how you got there.

> "We cut onboarding time in half. Here's what we changed."

This suits research, results updates and pitches. It respects the viewer's time and builds trust.

### 3. Start with a question

Ask something the viewer can answer in their head.

> "How long does it take a new starter to find the leave policy?"

Keep it specific. A broad question like "Have you ever wondered about productivity?" falls flat.

### 4. Start with a short story

One or two sentences about a real moment.

> "Last March, a nurse on night shift couldn't find the updated hand hygiene steps. She had the old poster, not the new one."

Stories work when they are short and directly connected to your point. Avoid long anecdotes that only pay off later.

### 5. Start with a surprising, true detail

A specific fact from your own work, not an invented statistic.

> "Our support team answered the same password question 40 times last week."

Only use numbers you can stand behind. If you don't have one, use a problem or question instead.

## A before-and-after opening

**Before**

- Slide 1: Title, "Research Findings 2026"
- Slide 2: "About me", with five bullet points
- Slide 3: "Agenda", with six items
- Slide 4: "Background", with a dense paragraph

Narration for the first minute: thanks, introductions, agenda, and the history of the project.

**After**

- Slide 1: Headline, "Shorter reminder emails got more replies"
  Speaker note: *We tested shorter reminder emails this year. They got more replies than the long ones. In the next five minutes, I'll show you what we tried and what you can use tomorrow.*
- Slide 2: Headline, "Three things we tested"
  Speaker note: *We changed three things: length, subject line and send time. Let's take them one at a time.*

The viewer knows the point and the plan in under twenty seconds. The bio can sit on the final slide.

## Make the first slide work hard

Your first slide is also your thumbnail in many places. Make it count:

- A headline that states the point or the problem.
- One strong visual or plenty of white space.
- Large text that reads on a phone.
- Your name and organisation in small type, if needed.

## Tell people what they'll get

After the hook, give a short promise of what's coming:

- "In the next five minutes, you'll see three changes you can make this week."
- "By the end, you'll know which form to use and when."

This replaces the agenda slide. It is shorter and more useful, because it is framed around the viewer's benefit, not your section headings.

## Openings for narrated video

When the opening is read by a narrator voice, a few extra habits help:

- **Keep the first sentence short.** It sets the rhythm.
- **Avoid tricky words early.** If a name or acronym may be mispronounced, introduce it a little later.
- **Match words to the screen.** The first spoken line should reflect the first slide's headline.
- **Don't open with a long silence.** Give the first slide a spoken line in its notes so it never sits there silent.

## Quick checklist

- The first sentence names a problem, result, question, story or concrete detail.
- No agenda, bio or housekeeping in the first thirty seconds.
- The first slide has a headline that makes a point.
- A one-line promise of what the viewer will get.
- Spoken words match what's on screen.
