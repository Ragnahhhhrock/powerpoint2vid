An explainer video can reach people a slide deck or a long document misses. It can also shut people out if it relies on one sense, one language level or one way of learning. Accessible explainer videos are built so more people can follow them: people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people who are blind or have low vision, people reading in a second language, and people with learning differences.

This post looks at what explainer videos do well for accessibility and inclusion, where they fall short, and the practical steps that close the gap.

## Why explainer videos can be more inclusive than text alone

Text-only material asks a lot of the reader. It assumes strong reading skills, good vision, time and focus. An explainer video can spread the load across more than one channel.

- **Narration plus visuals.** Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. Some people lean on the voice, some on the image.
- **Pace set by the viewer.** People can pause, rewind and rewatch without anyone noticing.
- **Captions.** People who are deaf or hard of hearing, people in noisy places, and people reading in a second language can follow along.
- **Consistency.** Everyone gets the same accurate explanation, not a version that depends on who they asked.

For educators, this supports the principle behind Universal Design for Learning: offer more than one way in to the same content.

## Where explainer videos can exclude people

Video is not automatically accessible. Common problems include:

- **No captions,** so the narration is lost to anyone who cannot hear it.
- **Meaning shown only visually,** such as "as you can see here" with no description of what is on screen.
- **Low contrast** or small text that is hard to read.
- **Colour as the only signal,** such as red for bad and green for good.
- **Fast pacing** that leaves no time to read or process.
- **Flashing or rapid motion,** which can be uncomfortable or unsafe for some viewers.
- **Jargon** that assumes background knowledge.

Each of these is fixable, mostly at the slide and script stage.

## Building accessible explainer videos from slides

### Write narration that describes what matters on screen

If a slide shows a chart, say what the chart shows. If it shows a diagram, describe the relationship. This helps blind and low-vision viewers, and it helps everyone else too.

- Before: "As you can see, this one is much higher."
- After: "Enrolments in the online course are higher than in the on-campus course in every semester shown."

### Caption everything

Captions are the single most important accessibility feature for video. PowerPoint2Vid can burn captions into the video sentence by sentence, so they always show, on any player. Note that there is no separate caption file export. If your platform requires a caption file, such as SRT or VTT, you will need to create one with another tool. For more on captions, see [Captions on Presentation Videos: Why and How](/blog/captions-on-presentation-videos-why-and-how).

### Use clear contrast and large text

Aim for WCAG AA contrast: 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Keep body text at 24pt or larger for video. Use a plain sans-serif font.

### Never rely on colour alone

Add a label, an icon or a pattern alongside colour. "Above target" in text next to a green bar works for everyone.

### Keep motion gentle

Choose calmer motion for content that needs focus. Clean and Minimal cinematic use gentle fades and slow camera movement. Avoid rapid flashing in any images or recordings you include.

### Use plain language

Short sentences. Common words. Explain terms the first time. Plain language helps people reading in a second language, people with cognitive disabilities, and people who are simply tired.

### Give people time

Let each slide stay on screen long enough to read. Narration timing helps, and the minimum seconds per slide setting gives text-heavy slides extra time. A slightly slower speaking speed can help with dense content.

## A quick example: one slide, made inclusive

**Original slide.** Headline: "Results". A pie chart in red, amber and green. Narration: "Here are the results. Green is good."

**Inclusive version.** Headline: "Most students passed the first assessment". A bar chart with direct labels: "Passed", "Resubmit", "Not yet submitted". Contrast checked. Captions on.

Narration:

> "Most students passed the first assessment. The longest bar shows students who passed. A smaller group needs to resubmit. A few have not submitted yet. If you're in either of those groups, the next slide explains what to do."

Now the message works with sound or without, with full colour vision or without, and for viewers who need a moment to read.

## What explainer videos cannot replace

Be honest about the limits. A video may not suit everyone, so offer an alternative where it matters:

- A text transcript or summary for people who prefer to read or use a screen reader.
- The original slides, shared in an accessible format.
- A way to ask questions, because a video cannot answer follow-ups.

Accessibility is about choice. The video is one good option among several.

## Key takeaways

- Narration plus visuals gives people more than one way to understand
- Captions are essential; burn them in so they always show
- Describe what matters on screen in the narration
- Meet WCAG AA contrast and keep text large
- Never use colour as the only signal
- Use plain language and give people time
- Offer a transcript or summary alongside the video
