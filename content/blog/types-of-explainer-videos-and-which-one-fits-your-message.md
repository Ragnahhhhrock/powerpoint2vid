"Explainer video" covers a lot of ground. A cartoon about a banking app, a screen recording of a software setup, a founder talking to camera and a narrated slide deck are all explainers. They just suit different jobs. Knowing the main types of explainer videos helps you pick the one that fits your message, your audience and your budget, instead of defaulting to whatever you saw last.

This post walks through the common types, what each is good at, and a simple way to choose.

## What every explainer has in common

Whatever the format, a good explainer does the same few things:

- Answers one question or explains one idea.
- Starts with the viewer's problem, not the product.
- Combines words and pictures, so the viewer hears and sees the point.
- Ends with a clear next step.

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. Every type below uses that idea in a different way. ## The main types of explainer videos

### 1. Animated character videos

Illustrated characters act out a story: a frustrated customer, a problem, a solution, a happy ending.

- **Good for:** Abstract services, consumer apps, emotional stories, broad audiences.
- **Watch out for:** Usually needs illustrators, animators and a scriptwriter. Changes later can mean redoing scenes.

### 2. Motion graphics

Shapes, icons, text and charts move to explain a concept. No characters, just clean visuals.

- **Good for:** Data-heavy ideas, processes, B2B products, explaining "how it works".
- **Watch out for:** Polished motion graphics take specialist skills and time.

### 3. Live-action videos

Real people, real places, filmed with a camera. Can include customer stories, product demos or a team on location.

- **Good for:** Building trust, showing physical products, human stories, brand personality.
- **Watch out for:** Needs filming, lighting, sound and editing. Hard to update without reshooting.

### 4. Talking-head videos

One person speaks to camera, sometimes with slides or graphics cut in.

- **Good for:** Founder messages, expert commentary, personal updates, building a connection with a known face.
- **Watch out for:** Depends heavily on the presenter's comfort on camera. Some viewers find long talking heads hard to stay with. See [animated slide videos vs talking-head videos](/blog/animated-slide-videos-vs-talking-head-videos) for a closer comparison.

### 5. Screencasts and product demos

A recording of a screen, with someone narrating as they click through software.

- **Good for:** Software how-tos, setup guides, feature walkthroughs, support answers.
- **Watch out for:** Goes out of date whenever the interface changes. Can be hard to follow if the narrator moves too fast.

### 6. Whiteboard and hand-drawn videos

A hand sketches the story on a whiteboard or paper as a narrator explains.

- **Good for:** Education, step-by-step reasoning, ideas that build piece by piece.
- **Watch out for:** The drawing style can feel dated if overused. Production takes time.

### 7. Narrated slide videos

A slide deck with a voiceover, animated reveals and transitions, exported as a video.

- **Good for:** Training, lectures, onboarding, sales walkthroughs, report summaries, anything that already lives in a deck.
- **Watch out for:** Can feel flat if the slides are crowded or the narration reads the slide word for word. Good slide design matters.

## Comparing the types at a glance

| Type | Best for | Effort to make | Easy to update? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Animated character | Stories, consumer brands | High | Hard |
| Motion graphics | Concepts, data, processes | Medium to high | Moderate |
| Live-action | Trust, physical products | High | Hard |
| Talking head | Personal messages, expertise | Low to medium | Moderate (reshoot) |
| Screencast | Software how-tos | Low to medium | Moderate (re-record) |
| Whiteboard | Teaching, step-by-step ideas | Medium | Hard |
| Narrated slides | Training, lectures, sales, reports | Low | Easy (edit the deck) |

## How to choose the right type

Ask four questions.

### What is the message?

- An emotional story or brand promise suits animation or live-action.
- A process or concept suits motion graphics, whiteboard or narrated slides.
- A software task suits a screencast.
- A personal message suits a talking head.

### Who is watching, and where?

- Prospects on your homepage may expect a polished brand piece.
- Employees, students and existing customers usually care more about clarity than polish.
- Social viewers often watch with the sound off, so captions and strong visuals matter more than voice.

### How often will it change?

If the content changes every quarter (policies, pricing, product steps), choose a format you can update quickly. Re-filming or re-animating every few months gets expensive fast.

### What do you already have?

If you already have a well-made deck, a narrated slide video is the shortest path to a finished explainer. If you have strong product footage, a demo may be quicker.

## Example: one company, three types

A small HR software company might use three formats for three jobs:

- **Homepage:** A short motion-graphics piece made by an agency, headline "Payroll that runs itself, so you can run the business."
- **Onboarding:** A series of narrated slide videos built from the existing training deck, one per setup step. Updated whenever a screen changes.
- **Founder update:** A quick talking-head video to customers explaining a pricing change.

No single type does every job well. Matching type to message is the point.

## Key takeaways

- Every explainer combines words and pictures to answer one question.
- Animation and live-action suit stories and brand; screencasts suit software tasks; talking heads suit personal messages.
- Narrated slides suit content that already lives in a deck and changes often.
- Choose by message, audience, update frequency and what you already have.
