Most research is read by a small circle of specialists. A short research explainer video can carry the same findings to students, practitioners, funders, journalists and the public, without dumbing them down. The good news is you probably already have the raw material: a conference deck, a lab meeting presentation or a thesis defence.

This playbook walks through turning those slides into a clear, narrated explainer that runs a few minutes and makes one point well.

## Why researchers are making explainer videos

There are plenty of practical reasons to add a video to your research outputs:

- **Reach beyond your field.** A video can be shared on a university news page, a lab website, social media or in a newsletter.
- **Engagement and impact reporting.** Many institutions and funders ask researchers to show how they've communicated their work beyond journals.
- **Teaching.** A short explainer of your own study makes a strong resource for students in your unit.
- **Recruitment.** Explaining a project clearly helps attract participants, collaborators and research students.

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. A narrated slide video is a direct application of that idea.

## Step 1: Find the one-sentence finding

Before you touch the slides, write the finding as a single plain sentence. Not the title of the paper. The thing you found.

**Paper title:** "Associations between urban canopy cover and summer surface temperatures in metropolitan residential precincts"

**One-sentence finding:** "Streets with more tree cover stay noticeably cooler on hot summer days."

If you can't get it into one sentence, your explainer will struggle. Ask a colleague outside your field to read it back to you. If they can't, simplify again.

## Step 2: Build a short story around it

A conference deck is organised for peers: background, methods, results, discussion. An explainer for a broader audience needs a different order. Use situation, problem, answer, proof, next step:

1. **Situation:** What's the everyday context? ("Cities get hotter in summer.")
2. **Problem:** Why does it matter? ("Heat affects health, energy bills and how people use their streets.")
3. **Answer:** What did you find? ("Streets with more tree cover stay cooler.")
4. **Proof:** How do you know? One clear chart, one sentence on method.
5. **Next step:** What should someone do or think differently? ("Planting decisions can make a measurable difference to local heat.")

That's often six to ten slides. Not forty.

## Step 3: Cut the methods down to one slide

Your methods were hard work, and peers need them. A general audience needs to trust them, not replicate them. One slide is usually enough:

- What you measured
- Where and over what period
- Roughly how much data

Put the detail in the video description with a link to the paper. Anyone who wants more can follow it.

## Step 4: Redesign the key chart

Conference charts are often dense: multiple panels, error bars, small axis labels and a legend in the corner. For video, rebuild the one chart that proves your finding.

- One chart, one message
- Highlight the series or bar that matters, grey out the rest
- Label directly on the chart instead of using a legend
- Use a headline that states the result, not the variable names

**Before headline:** "Fig. 3: LST vs canopy % by precinct"

**After headline:** "More tree cover, cooler streets"

Keep body text large enough to read on a phone screen, which means 24pt or larger for anything that matters. For chart design advice, see [designing charts people understand in five seconds](/blog/designing-charts-people-understand-in-five-seconds).

## Step 5: Write speaker notes for a curious non-expert

The narration comes from your speaker notes, read aloud by the browser's built-in text-to-speech voice. Write for someone smart who isn't in your field.

- Replace jargon with plain words, or explain it once
- Say numbers the way people say them
- Spell out abbreviations, or avoid them
- Keep sentences short

Here's a sample note for the proof slide:

> We measured street surface temperatures across a range of suburbs during summer, and compared them with how much tree canopy each street had. The pattern was clear. Streets with more trees were cooler, and the difference was large enough to notice when you're walking outside.

If a technical term sounds odd in the preview, spell it the way it should be said in the notes, or rephrase it.

## Step 6: Choose a style and add captions

Two styles tend to suit research content:

- **Minimal cinematic:** calm, considered motion with lower-third captions. Good for serious topics.
- **Paper & ink:** a handmade, notebook feel. Good for teaching and public-facing explainers.

Turn on burned-in captions. They help viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, people reading in a second language and anyone watching with the sound off. Captions are part of the video image, so they travel wherever the file goes.

## Step 7: Share it and link it

Once exported, put the video where your audiences already are:

- Your lab or project web page
- The university's research news or media team
- A unit in your LMS for students
- Social channels, with the one-sentence finding as the post text
- Your conference or journal profile, if it allows video

In every description, include the full citation and a link to the paper.

## Quick checklist

- One-sentence finding written and tested on a non-expert
- Deck rebuilt as situation, problem, answer, proof, next step
- Methods reduced to one slide
- One chart, one message, direct labels
- Notes in plain spoken language
- Captions on, sensible style chosen
- Citation and paper link in every description
