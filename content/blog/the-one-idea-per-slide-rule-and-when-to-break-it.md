Of all the presentation design tips you'll hear, one does more work than the rest: one idea per slide. If a slide needs two headlines, it's two slides. This post explains why the rule works, how to apply it to a crowded deck, and the few times it's right to break it.

## What "one idea" actually means

An idea is a single point you want the audience to take away. It's not a topic. "Customer feedback" is a topic. "Customers want faster delivery more than lower prices" is an idea.

A good test: can you write the slide's point as one sentence? If you can, that sentence is probably your headline. If you need "and" to join two separate claims, you have two slides.

## Why the rule works

### The audience knows where to look

When a slide carries one point, people know what they're meant to notice. When it carries four, they start reading everything at once and stop listening to you.

### You talk about what's on screen

A slide with three ideas sits behind you while you discuss the first one. The other two are a distraction. One idea per slide keeps what people see in step with what they hear.

### It works better as video

This matters even more when a deck becomes a video. Each slide stays up for as long as its narration lasts. A slide covering three ideas becomes a long, static shot. Three slides covering one idea each give the viewer a change of picture every time the topic moves on.

### It's easier to update

When one point changes, you edit one slide. You don't have to rebalance a crowded layout.

## Presentation design tips for splitting a crowded slide

Here's a typical slide from a sales update.

**Before: one slide**

Headline: "Q3 update"
- Revenue up on last quarter
- Two new enterprise customers signed
- Churn rose in the small-business segment
- Hiring two more account managers

That's four ideas under a headline that says nothing. Here's how to split it.

**After: four slides**

1. "Revenue grew again in Q3"
2. "Two enterprise customers signed this quarter"
3. "Small-business churn is rising and needs attention"
4. "We're hiring two account managers to cover the growth"

Each slide now has a headline that makes a point and room for a chart, a logo or a single number. The detail moves into the speaker notes.

### A simple process

1. **List the points.** Go slide by slide and write down each separate claim.
2. **Turn each point into a sentence.** That becomes the headline.
3. **Give each sentence its own slide.**
4. **Choose one visual per slide.** A chart, a photo, a diagram, a big number or nothing at all.
5. **Move the explanation into the notes.** That's what you'll say, or what the narration will read.

### "But that's too many slides"

More slides isn't the same as a longer presentation. Four simple slides at thirty seconds each take the same time as one crowded slide at two minutes. The difference is that the audience can follow along.

## When to break the rule

The rule is a default, not a law. A few situations call for more than one idea on screen.

### Comparisons

If the point is the difference between two things, they need to be seen together. "Plan A vs Plan B" on one slide is still one idea: the comparison.

### Summaries and agendas

A slide that lists the three sections coming up, or recaps the three points just made, is meant to show several things at once. Keep it short and use it sparingly.

### Reference slides

Some decks are read, not presented: a pricing table, a timeline or a set of contact details. These can hold more, because the reader sets the pace. If the deck is going to become a video, though, consider whether that slide needs more time on screen or belongs in a handout instead.

### Process diagrams

A five-step process is one idea if the point is the process as a whole. Show it all, then use builds to highlight each step as you talk about it.

## Making the rule work with animation

Builds let you show several items without overwhelming people. Reveal one point at a time, as you talk about it. The slide still ends up with several elements, but the audience meets them one by one.

## Quick checklist

- Can each slide's point be written as one sentence?
- Does the headline state that point, not just the topic?
- Is there one main visual per slide?
- Has detail moved into the speaker notes?
- Are comparisons and summaries the only multi-idea slides?
- On fuller slides, do builds reveal points one at a time?

## Key takeaways

- One idea per slide keeps attention where you want it.
- Split crowded slides by turning each point into a headline.
- More slides doesn't mean a longer talk.
- Break the rule for comparisons, summaries, reference slides and processes.
