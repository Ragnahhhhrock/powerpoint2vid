Every training team eventually has this debate. Should the new process be a written guide or a video? The honest answer is that they do different jobs. Thinking about video vs documentation as a choice between rivals leads to gaps. Thinking about them as partners leads to training people actually use.

This post sets out what each does well, when to pick one over the other, and how to produce both from the same source without doubling your workload.

## What written documentation does well

Written docs are hard to beat when people need to find, check or copy something.

- **Scanning.** Readers can jump to the section they need in seconds.
- **Search.** Text is searchable inside your intranet, wiki or knowledge base.
- **Reference.** Exact values, policy wording, codes and links are easy to copy.
- **Quick edits.** Changing a sentence takes moments.
- **Reading at your own pace.** People who prefer to read, or who are reading in a second language, can slow down.

Documentation is the right home for anything people will return to: policy text, step-by-step reference, contact lists and detailed rules.

## What explainer videos do well

Video is strongest when people need to understand something for the first time.

- **Context and reasoning.** Narration can explain why a process exists, not just what it is.
- **Showing a sequence.** A diagram that builds step by step is easier to follow than a paragraph describing it.
- **Attention.** A short narrated video gives a new idea a clear beginning, middle and end.
- **Consistency.** Every new starter hears the same explanation, in the same words.
- **Accessibility.** Narration helps people who find dense text hard going, and captions help people who are deaf, hard of hearing or watching with the sound off.

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. That is the core strength of a narrated slide video.

## Video vs documentation: a side-by-side view

| Need | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| First introduction to a new process | Video | Builds understanding with narration and visuals |
| Looking up a specific rule or value | Documentation | Fast to scan, search and copy |
| Explaining why a policy changed | Video | Narration carries reasoning and tone |
| Step-by-step reference at the desk | Documentation | Readers move at their own pace |
| Onboarding a group with the same message | Video | Everyone gets the same explanation |
| Legal or exact policy wording | Documentation | The text is the authority |
| Reaching people across shifts and time zones | Both | Watch or read when it suits |

## The best answer is usually both

Most training topics benefit from a short video and a written reference. The video introduces and explains. The documentation supports and confirms.

A pattern that works well:

1. **A short video** of two to five minutes that explains what is changing, why, and what people need to do.
2. **A written page** with the detail: steps, rules, links, contacts.
3. **A link between them.** The video ends by pointing to the page. The page opens with the video.

For more on using video inside an organisation, see [Explainer Videos for Internal Communication](/blog/explainer-videos-for-internal-communication).

## Produce both from one source

The fear with doing both is double the work and two versions drifting apart. A slide deck with good speaker notes solves most of that.

- The **slides** carry the visuals for the video.
- The **speaker notes** become the narration and, lightly edited, the written guide.
- When something changes, you **edit the deck once**, create the video again and update the page from the same notes.

Here is an example for a new expense policy.

**Slide headline:** Receipts now go in the app, not by email

**Speaker note:**

"From the first of February, please upload every receipt in the expenses app. Don't email them to finance any more. Take a photo, attach it to the expense, and submit. Your manager will see it straight away."

**Written guide, adapted from the same note:**

*Submitting receipts (from 1 February)*

1. Take a photo of the receipt.
2. Open the expenses app and attach it to the expense.
3. Submit. Your manager is notified automatically.
4. Do not email receipts to finance.

The words match because they came from the same place. When the policy changes again, you update one set of notes. For a fuller example of this approach, see [Employee Onboarding Videos From Your Existing PowerPoint](/blog/employee-onboarding-videos-from-your-existing-powerpoint).

## When to skip the video

Video is not always worth it.

- **Tiny changes.** A new phone number does not need a video.
- **Pure reference.** A table of codes is better as a table.
- **Very frequent changes.** If something changes weekly, keep it in text.

Save video for topics people need to understand, not just look up.

## Key takeaways

- Documentation is best for scanning, searching and exact reference.
- Video is best for first explanations, reasoning and showing sequences.
- Most topics work best with a short video and a written page, linked together.
- Use one deck and its speaker notes as the source for both.
- Skip video for tiny, reference-only or constantly changing content.
