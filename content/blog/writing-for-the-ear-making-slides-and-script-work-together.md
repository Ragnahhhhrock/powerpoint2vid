Most lecturers write slides first and work out what to say later. That works in a live room, where you can read faces and adjust. In a recorded lecture or narrated video, the script carries far more weight. Good presentation script writing means writing for the ear, then designing slides that support what students hear rather than competing with it.

This post covers how spoken writing differs from written writing, how to split the work between script and slide, and a simple process for drafting both together.

## Why the ear needs different writing

Readers can skim, re-read and slow down. Listeners can't. They hear each sentence once, at the speaker's pace, in order. That changes the rules.

Writing for the eye often uses:

- Long sentences with several clauses.
- Brackets, abbreviations and footnotes.
- Precise numbers like 37.4%.
- Formal phrasing and passive voice.

Writing for the ear works better with:

- Short sentences, one idea each.
- Plain words and contractions.
- Rounded numbers, said the way people say them.
- Repetition of key terms, so listeners don't lose the thread.
- Signposts like "first", "the second reason" and "so what does this mean?"

Research on multimedia learning, such as Richard Mayer's work, suggests people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone, and that spoken narration with visuals is often easier to follow than the same words printed on screen next to the visuals. That's the core idea behind this post: let the ear take the words, and let the eye take the picture.

## Presentation script writing: the split between slide and script

The most common problem in narrated lectures is a slide covered in text, read aloud word for word. Students try to read and listen at the same time, and both suffer.

A better split:

| On the slide | In the script |
|---|---|
| The headline point, as a short sentence | The explanation and reasoning |
| One visual: diagram, chart, image or key term | What to notice in the visual |
| A few key words, if any | Examples, stories and analogies |
| Labels on a diagram | How the parts connect |

The slide is what students should remember. The script is how they get there.

## How to write the script

### Say the point first

Start each slide's narration with its main idea. Then explain. Then, if useful, repeat the point in different words.

### Keep sentences short

If a sentence has a comma, a "which" and an "and", it's probably two or three sentences. Read it aloud. If you run out of breath, split it.

### Use spoken numbers

- Written: "Retention increased from 62.3% to 71.8%."
- Spoken: "Retention went up from about sixty per cent to over seventy."

Put the exact figures on the slide if they matter. Say the rounded version.

### Explain abbreviations the first time

"Cognitive load theory, or CLT" the first time. After that, either the full term or the abbreviation consistently.

### Point with words, not gestures

In a video, "this one here" means nothing. Say what you're pointing at: "the red line at the top of the chart".

### Signpost the structure

Tell students where they are. "There are three reasons. The first is..." "That's the second reason. The last one is the most important." Signposts are easy to skip in writing and essential when listening.

## A before and after

Here's a slide from a first-year statistics lecture.

**Before**

Slide headline: "Sampling Bias"

Slide body:

- Occurs when certain members of a population are systematically more likely to be selected than others
- Results in a non-representative sample
- E.g. voluntary response surveys, convenience sampling
- Leads to invalid inferences about the population

Script: the lecturer reads the four bullets.

**After**

Slide headline: "A biased sample gives you the wrong answer, confidently"

Slide body: a simple picture of a crowd, with a small cluster circled and labelled "who answered".

Script:

> "Sampling bias happens when some people are more likely to end up in your sample than others. Here's a common example. You post a survey online and ask people to reply. Who replies? Usually the people with strong opinions. So your results look clear, but they only describe the people who chose to answer. That's the danger. A biased sample doesn't look wrong. It just quietly gives you the wrong answer."

The definition is still there, in plain words. The example does the teaching. The slide gives students one image to hang the idea on.

## A process for drafting slides and script together

1. **List your points.** One line per idea, in order. These become your slide headlines.
2. **Write the script for each point.** Out loud, or as if you were. Don't look at slide design yet.
3. **Read the script aloud.** Fix anything you stumble over. Cut anything you wouldn't say to a student in office hours.
4. **Design the slide last.** Ask: what one visual or phrase would help someone hearing this script? Put that on the slide.
5. **Put the script in the speaker notes.** That way it travels with the slide and is ready for recording or narration.
6. **Listen back.** Play the deck with the notes read aloud. Check that what students hear and what they see line up.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the script as an essay.** Long, formal sentences are hard to follow by ear.
- **Putting the script on the slide.** If the slide has every word you say, students will read instead of listen.
- **Never saying the point.** Describing a chart without saying what it shows.
- **Skipping signposts.** Listeners lose track of where they are in the argument.
- **Not listening back.** Problems you'd never see on the page are obvious when you hear them.

## Quick checklist

- Each slide has a headline that states the point.
- The script says the point first, then explains.
- Sentences are short and easy to say in one breath.
- Numbers are rounded and spoken naturally.
- Abbreviations are explained the first time.
- Visuals are described in words, not just pointed at.
- The slide carries one visual or phrase; the script carries the explanation.
- The script lives in the speaker notes.
