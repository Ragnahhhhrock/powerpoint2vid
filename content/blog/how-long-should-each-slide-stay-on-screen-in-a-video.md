Slide timing for video is different from timing a live talk. In a room, you can pause, read the audience and move on when people look ready. In a video, every slide gets a fixed amount of time, and viewers can't ask you to slow down. Get the timing right and the video feels calm and clear. Get it wrong and it either drags or rushes past the point.

This guide gives you simple rules for deciding how long each slide should stay on screen, with examples from training content.

## The short answer: let the narration decide

The best timing for a slide is "as long as it takes to say what the slide is about, plus a beat". If the narration for a slide takes 20 seconds, the slide should be on screen for about 20 seconds, with a short pause before the next one.

That is why narrated slide videos usually feel better than silent ones with fixed timings. The words set the rhythm, and the visuals follow.

There is no single correct number of seconds. A title slide might need four seconds. A slide walking through a three-step process might need forty.

## Slide timing for video: practical ranges

These are starting points, not rules. Adjust them after you watch the preview.

| Slide type | Typical time on screen | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Title or section divider | 3 to 6 seconds | Long enough to read, short enough to keep momentum |
| Single headline with one image | 8 to 20 seconds | One idea, one or two sentences of narration |
| Process or step-by-step slide | 20 to 45 seconds | Each step needs its own sentence |
| Chart or data slide | 15 to 30 seconds | Viewers need time to find the point you are describing |
| Closing or next-step slide | 6 to 12 seconds | Give people time to note a link or action |

If a slide needs more than about 45 seconds of narration, that is often a sign it is carrying two ideas. Split it.

## Signs a slide is on screen too long

- The narration is still going but the slide has nothing new to look at.
- You find yourself adding "as you can see" to fill time.
- The slide contains a dense paragraph that the voice reads word for word.

**Fixes:** split the slide into two, reveal elements one at a time as the narration reaches them, or move detail out of the slide and into a linked document.

## Signs a slide is too short

- Viewers can't finish reading the headline before it changes.
- A chart disappears before anyone could find the highlighted bar.
- A title slide flashes past with no narration at all.

**Fixes:** add a sentence of narration, or hold the slide a little longer so it gets a fair look.

## Example: re-timing a compliance slide

Here is a training slide about reporting a data breach, as it often starts out.

**Before**

- Headline: "Data breach reporting"
- Six bullet points covering who to tell, how fast, what to record, what not to do, which form to use and where to find it
- Speaker note: one long paragraph, about 70 seconds when read aloud

In the video, the slide sits still for over a minute while the voice works through everything. Viewers drift.

**After**

The content becomes three slides.

1. "Report a suspected breach to the privacy team within one hour"
   Note: *If you think personal information has been exposed, tell the privacy team within one hour. Don't wait until you're sure.*
2. "Record what happened, when, and who was affected"
   Note: *Write down what happened, when you noticed it and whose information may be involved. Short notes are fine.*
3. "Use the breach form on the intranet, not email"
   Note: *Use the breach form on the intranet. Please don't send details by email, because email can spread the problem.*

Each slide now runs about 15 to 20 seconds. The total length is similar, but the viewer gets a new visual every time the topic changes. That is the real goal of good timing.

## Use builds to pace long slides

Some slides genuinely need time, such as a diagram with four parts. Instead of showing everything at once, reveal each part as the narration reaches it. The slide stays on screen longer, but there is always something new to look at.

## Write notes with timing in mind

Narration length is the main lever you have, so write it carefully. A few habits help:

- One to three sentences per simple slide.
- One sentence per step on a process slide.
- Say numbers the way people say them: "about two thousand", not "1,984".
- Read each note aloud once. If you run out of breath, it's too long.

## Quick checklist

- Every slide has narration that matches what is on screen.
- No slide runs much past 45 seconds without new visuals.
- Title and divider slides are short but readable.
- Charts get time for the viewer to find the point.
- Very short slides have enough narration to be read comfortably.
- You have watched the full video once at normal speed.
