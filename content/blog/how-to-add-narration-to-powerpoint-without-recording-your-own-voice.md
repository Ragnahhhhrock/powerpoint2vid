Plenty of people need to add voiceover to PowerPoint but don't want to record their own voice. Maybe you don't have a quiet room. Maybe the deck changes every month and re-recording is a chore. Maybe you just don't like hearing yourself. There's a practical alternative: write the narration as speaker notes and let a voice read it for you.

This post explains the options, how narration from speaker notes works, and how to write notes that sound natural when read aloud.

## Your options for narration

There are three common ways to narrate a deck. Each has a place.

| Option | Good for | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Record yourself in PowerPoint | Personal messages, teaching where your own voice matters | Needs a quiet space and a decent microphone; any change means re-recording that slide |
| Hire a voice artist | High-profile videos, brand campaigns | Costs money and time; updates need another booking |
| Narration from speaker notes | Training, onboarding, explainers that change often | Some words need spelling help |

PowerPoint can record narration and timings as you present, then export a video. That works well if you are comfortable recording. This post focuses on the third option.

## How to add voiceover to PowerPoint from speaker notes

The idea is simple: what you type into speaker notes becomes the narration.

### Step 1: Write a note for every slide

Open the notes pane under each slide and write what a presenter would say. Keep it to a few sentences for most slides.

### Step 2: Load the deck into a converter that reads notes

With PowerPoint2Vid, you add your .pptx and the speaker notes become the narration. Google Slides decks need exporting as .pptx first.

### Step 3: Choose what gets narrated

Speaker notes are the script, so decide how each slide should sound. A slide with a full note gets a full explanation. A slide with a short note stays brief. Where a slide has no note, write one, or the narration has little to work from.

### Step 4: Pick a voice and speed

Choose a voice that suits your audience. Adjust the speaking speed until it feels comfortable. For training content, slightly slower than normal conversation is often easier to follow.

### Step 5: Watch and listen

Play the video through. Note any slide where a word is mispronounced, a sentence runs too long, or the slide changes awkwardly.

### Step 6: Export

When you're happy, download the video as an MP4 (WebM is also available). If the deck is internal or sensitive, check your organisation's policy on using online tools before uploading it.

## Writing notes that sound natural

A narration voice reads exactly what you type. Writing for the ear makes a big difference.

### Short sentences

Long sentences with several clauses sound flat when read aloud by a voice. Break them up.

**Before:**

> The new leave request process, which replaces the paper form that was previously used in most departments, requires employees to submit their request through the HR portal at least two weeks in advance, unless it is an emergency.

**After:**

> Leave requests now go through the HR portal. This replaces the old paper form. Please submit your request at least two weeks ahead. Emergencies are the exception.

### Say numbers the way people say them

- "2–3 days" may be read oddly. Write "two to three days".
- "$1,500" may be fine, but "fifteen hundred dollars" is predictable.
- "Q3" might be read as "queue three". Write "the third quarter".

### Spell out tricky words

Acronyms, product names and place names can trip up a voice. Listen for the word in the video. If it's wrong, spell it the way it sounds, such as "S-Q-L" or "sequel", whichever your team says.

### Use punctuation for pauses

A full stop gives a natural pause. A comma gives a shorter one. Use them on purpose. If two ideas run together, split them into separate sentences.

## Why trainers like this approach

For L&D and HR teams, narrating from speaker notes has some practical benefits:

- **Updates are quick.** Change a sentence in the notes and export again. No re-recording.
- **The voice stays consistent.** Every module can use the same voice, even if several people write the content.
- **Nobody has to be on camera or on mic.** Subject matter experts can write notes without recording anything.
- **The deck gets upgraded, not just recorded.** The story is tightened and each slide becomes an animated scene, so plain training slides look produced.

## Limits to know about

- PowerPoint2Vid doesn't record your microphone and doesn't do voice cloning. Narration comes from your speaker notes.
- Some names and acronyms need respelling in the notes to sound right.
- If you need extra editing, you can bring the downloaded video into a video editor.

## Quick checklist

- Every slide has speaker notes, or you've chosen the narration mode deliberately.
- Sentences are short and written for the ear.
- Numbers, acronyms and names are written the way they should sound.
- Voice and speed checked by watching the video.
- Captions turned on if viewers may watch without sound.
- Video played back once with sound on before sharing.
