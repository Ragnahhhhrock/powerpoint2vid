You already have the raw material for a month of social content. It's sitting in your sales deck, your launch deck and last quarter's webinar slides. Turning a presentation to social video is mostly an editing job: pick the slides that carry one idea each, tighten the words, and export short clips people can follow with the sound off.

This playbook walks through the process step by step, with a worked example at the end.

## Why turning a presentation to social video works

A good deck has already done the hard thinking. The message is agreed, the visuals are on brand and the numbers have been checked. That is most of what a social video needs.

Slides also suit the way people scroll:

- **One idea per frame.** A well-built slide says one thing, which is what a short clip needs.
- **Big text.** Slide headlines are already sized to be read from a distance, so they hold up on a phone.
- **On-brand by default.** Your template carries your colours and fonts, so every clip looks like it came from the same place.

## Step 1: Choose the slides, not the deck

Don't export the whole 30-slide deck and post it. Social clips work best when they are short and make a single point.

Go through the deck and tag slides that:

- Make a claim a stranger would care about.
- Show a before-and-after or a simple comparison.
- Answer a question customers ask often.
- Have a strong chart with one message.

Group them into small sets of three to six slides. Each set becomes one video. A 30-slide deck might give you five or six clips.

## Step 2: Build a short deck for each clip

Save a copy of the deck for each clip and delete everything else. Then shape each copy like a tiny story:

1. **Hook slide.** A headline that names the problem or the payoff.
2. **Two to four point slides.** One idea each.
3. **Close slide.** One action, such as "See the full demo at example.com/demo".

Keep the hook short. Something like "Three numbers every new café owner should track" works better than a title slide with your logo and the deck name.

## Step 3: Rewrite the headlines for the feed

Slide headlines written for a meeting room often lean on context the viewer doesn't have. Rewrite them so each one makes sense on its own.

**Before:** "Q3 results"
**After:** "Customers who finished onboarding stayed twice as long"

Use your own real numbers, and only where you can stand behind them. If a headline needs a footnote to be true, change the headline. For more on this, see our guide to [slide headlines that make a point](/blog/slide-headlines-that-make-a-point).

## Step 4: Write short speaker notes

If you narrate the clip, the speaker notes become the voiceover. For social, keep them tight: one or two sentences per slide.

Sample notes for a hook slide:

> Most new café owners track sales. Fewer track the three numbers that actually predict a good month. Here they are.

Plain words, short sentences, no jargon. Read them aloud once before you export.

## Step 5: Design for sound off

Plenty of people scroll with the sound muted. Plan for it from the start:

- Burn captions into the video so the narration appears on screen.
- Put the key point in the slide headline, not only in the voiceover.
- Use high-contrast text that reads on a small screen.
- Avoid long paragraphs. If a slide needs one, split it.

## Step 6: Pick a style that suits the platform

Motion style changes the feel of a clip more than most people expect. Some rough pairings:

| Goal | Style direction |
|---|---|
| Bold announcements, stats, launches | Fast cuts and shapes that land with energy |
| Calm product or brand story | Slow camera movement, soft reveals |
| Polished product reveal | Slide shown as a card on a dark stage |
| Friendly, approachable tips | Springy, playful motion on a warm background |
| Behind-the-scenes, craft, editorial | Paper texture, grain, hand-drawn touches |

Try two styles on the same short deck and compare. Pick the one that matches your brand voice, then stick with it so your clips look like a series.

## Step 7: Post with context

The video is only part of the post. Before you publish:

- Write a first line that works on its own, because many people won't expand the caption.
- Repeat the call to action in the post text.
- Add a short description of the video for accessibility where the platform allows it.
- Check the clip on a phone before posting. Small text is the most common problem.

Note on shape: slide decks are usually 16:9 landscape, and that is the shape your exported video will be. Landscape works well in feeds like LinkedIn and YouTube. If a platform favours vertical video, you can crop or reframe the file in a video editor afterwards, so design with that in mind by keeping key text near the centre.

## A worked example

Say you're the marketer at a small accounting software company. Your webinar deck has 40 slides. Here's how it could become a week of posts:

- **Monday:** "The five-minute month-end check" (four slides, a simple numbered process).
- **Tuesday:** "What changes when invoices go out the same day" (one chart, one point).
- **Wednesday:** "Three questions to ask before you switch accounting tools" (three slides, one question each).
- **Thursday:** A customer quote slide with a short narrated intro (only if the customer has approved its use).
- **Friday:** "See the full webinar" (a short recap with a link).

Each clip is a separate small deck. Each one uses the same style and caption setting, so the week looks like a set.

## Quick checklist

- One idea per clip, three to six slides each.
- A hook slide that names the problem or payoff.
- Headlines that stand alone without the rest of the deck.
- Short speaker notes, read aloud once.
- Burned-in captions for sound-off viewing.
- One consistent style across the series.
- A clear action in both the video and the post text.
