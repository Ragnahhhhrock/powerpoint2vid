Many conferences now ask speakers to send a recording ahead of time. Sometimes it is for a hybrid or online stream. Sometimes it is a backup in case the live connection drops. Either way, a pre-recorded conference talk loses the energy of a room, so it has to earn attention in other ways: tighter structure, cleaner slides and a script written to be heard.

This playbook walks through the process from brief to delivery.

## Read the brief carefully

Organisers usually send requirements. Read them twice and note:

- **Length.** Hard limits are common. A 12-minute slot means 12 minutes, not 14.
- **Format and resolution.** Many ask for MP4 at 1080p in 16:9.
- **Deadline.** Recordings are often due weeks before the event.
- **Captions.** Some conferences require them, and some want a caption file rather than burned-in text.
- **Speaker video.** Some want your face on screen. Others are happy with slides and voice.
- **Q&A.** Find out whether you will join live for questions after the recording plays.

If anything is unclear, ask. It is much easier to fix a misunderstanding before you record than after.

## Structure a pre-recorded conference talk for attention

A live audience forgives a slow start. A remote viewer with other tabs open does not. Get to the point early.

A simple structure that works:

1. **Hook (30 seconds).** The problem, in one sentence, and why it matters to this audience.
2. **The answer (1 minute).** Your main finding or argument, stated plainly.
3. **The evidence (most of the talk).** Three sections at most, each with one clear point.
4. **What it means (1 to 2 minutes).** Implications for the people watching.
5. **Next step (30 seconds).** Where to find the paper, the code, or you.

Stating the answer early may feel like giving away the ending. In a talk, it is the opposite. People follow the evidence more closely when they know where it leads. For more on this, see [How to Open a Presentation So People Keep Watching](/blog/how-to-open-a-presentation-so-people-keep-watching).

## Design slides for a small window

Your talk may be watched in a browser tile, on a laptop, or on a phone. Design for the smallest likely screen.

- One idea per slide.
- Headlines that state the point.
- Body text at 24pt or larger.
- Charts with one highlighted message and direct labels.
- Strong contrast between text and background.

Slides can change more often than in a live talk. A new visual every 20 to 40 seconds helps hold attention without feeling rushed.

## Write the script in your speaker notes

The script is where a recorded talk wins or loses. Write it in the speaker notes, slide by slide, the way you would say it.

Here is a sample for a research talk slide titled "Most errors happen at handover":

"We looked at every reported error over two years. The pattern was clear. Most of them happened at shift handover, not during the shift itself. That's the moment information gets passed from one person to another, and that's where it gets lost."

Notice the short sentences and spoken rhythm. No brackets, no abbreviations a voice might stumble on, and numbers written the way you would say them. For more detail, see [Writing a Voiceover Script: Pace, Pauses and Plain Words](/blog/writing-a-voiceover-script-pace-pauses-and-plain-words).

Read the whole script aloud once with a timer. If it runs long, cut whole points rather than talking faster.

## Choose how to record

You have a few options, and each suits different speakers.

| Approach | What it involves | Good when |
|---|---|---|
| Record yourself in PowerPoint | Speak over each slide using PowerPoint's own recording and export | You want your own voice and are comfortable recording |
| Camera plus slides | Record yourself on camera with slides alongside | The conference wants a speaker on screen |
| Text-to-speech from notes | A tool reads your speaker notes and records the slides | You want consistent narration, easy edits, or are presenting in a second language |

PowerPoint2Vid takes the third approach. It reads your speaker notes with your browser's built-in voices, animates the slides, and records a video in the browser. It does not record your own microphone or camera. If the conference wants your face or voice, use one of the other approaches, or check whether a narrated slide video is acceptable.

## Make it feel produced

Small touches make a recorded talk feel deliberate.

- **Pick a calm video style.** Minimal cinematic and Keynote launch both give a polished, conference-ready look. Clean works if you want the slides to speak for themselves.
- **Use gentle transitions.** A crossfade is rarely distracting.
- **Reveal shapes one by one** on diagram slides, so each part arrives as you describe it.
- **Burn in captions.** They help viewers with the sound off and those listening in a second language. If the organisers need a caption file such as SRT, you will need to make that separately.
- **Set a minimum time per slide** so title and section slides do not flash past.

Preview the full talk with voice. Watch it once as a viewer, not the author.

## Plan for the live Q&A

If you join live after the recording, prepare:

- Three questions you expect, with short answers.
- A backup slide or two with extra detail.
- A link to the paper or resources ready to paste into chat.

Watch your own recording one more time on the day. It helps you pick up where the talk left off.

## Quick checklist

- Requirements noted: length, format, captions, deadline, speaker video.
- Answer stated in the first two minutes.
- Slides built for a small screen.
- Script in speaker notes, timed aloud.
- Recording approach chosen to match the brief.
- Captions checked against the organisers' requirements.
- Q&A notes ready.
